Polish lullabies and nursery rhymes are often the very first Polish a child hears: a soft "a-a-a" at bedtime, a magpie cooking porridge in the palm of a hand, a crayfish creeping up an arm before it pinches. Generations of Polish grandmothers have used the same handful of songs, and many of them are old enough that nobody knows who first sang them.

If you are raising a bilingual child, or you are learning Polish yourself, these Polish lullabies are worth far more than their size suggests. They are short, rhythmic and full of the sounds, diminutives and grammar patterns that make Polish feel like Polish. Better still, most of them come with a game, a gesture or a tickle, which is exactly what keeps a toddler asking for "jeszcze raz!" (again!).

Below you will find eight traditional Polish lullabies and nursery rhymes, from *Aaa, kotki dwa* to *Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi*. For each one we give the traditional Polish text (with a note when versions differ), our own plain-English rendering, the words and grammar it teaches, and the game or gesture that goes with it. At the end there are practical tips for using them with kids who are learning Polish.

## Why Polish Lullabies Work So Well for Bilingual Kids

Children do not learn a language from word lists; they learn it from voices, rhythm and repetition. Polish lullabies sung every night give a child hundreds of repetitions of the same sentence patterns in a relaxed, happy setting. For a heritage child growing up outside Poland, that steady drip of Polish can matter a great deal.

Polish lullabies also carry the music of the language. The stress almost always falls on the second-to-last syllable, and singing makes that pattern obvious. Tricky sounds like *sz*, *cz*, *rz* and the nasal *ę* turn up again and again, so little mouths get regular practice without any drills.

Finally, Polish nursery rhymes are packed with diminutives: *kotek*, *płotek*, *sroczka*, *kaszka*, *łyżeczka*, *koniku*. Polish uses these affectionate "little" forms everywhere in family life, and rhymes are where children absorb them first. If you want to understand how those endings work, our guide to [Polish diminutives](/blog/polish-diminutives) explains the patterns behind *-ek*, *-ka* and *-eczka*.

## Kołysanki, Rymowanki and Zabawianki: Types of Polish Nursery Rhymes

Polish has several words for the songs and rhymes you will meet in this guide, and knowing them helps when you search for recordings or books of Polish lullabies.

- **Kołysanka**: a lullaby, from *kołysać* (to rock). A *kołyska* is a cradle, and Polish lullabies as a group are *kołysanki*.
- **Rymowanka**: a rhyme in general, the umbrella term for most Polish nursery rhymes.
- **Wyliczanka**: a counting-out rhyme, used to choose who is "it" in a game.
- **Zabawianka**: a play rhyme for babies and toddlers. Folklorists distinguish *zabawianki paluszkowe* (finger plays, like the magpie rhyme) and *husianki* (lap or knee-bouncing rhymes).
- **Klaskanka**: a clapping rhyme.

Polish ethnographers treat these play rhymes as a child's first contact with folklore. A study of the Dukla area in south-eastern Poland, published in 2023, recorded ten local variants of the magpie rhyme alone, which shows how alive Polish lullabies and play rhymes still are and how freely they change from village to village.

## Aaa, kotki dwa: The Classic Polish Lullaby

If you ask Polish parents to name one lullaby, many will start humming *Aaa, kotki dwa* ("Hush-a-bye, two kittens"). It is one of the best-known Polish lullabies, its author is unknown, and so many poets have written their own versions and parodies that the core lines below are really the shared starting point.

> A-a-a, kotki dwa,
> szaro-bure obydwa.
> Nic nie będą robiły,
> tylko dzieci bawiły.

**Meaning:** Hush, hush, two little cats, both of them grey and brownish. They won't do a single thing, except play with the children.

The long opening "a-a-a" is not a word at all: it is the sound Polish parents make while rocking a baby, a bit like English "shh". Many families stretch the song with extra verses or swap *dzieci* (children) for the child's name, and that is entirely in the spirit of the thing.

**What kotki dwa teaches:**
- *kotki* = little cats, kittens. *Kot* (cat) becomes *kotek* in the diminutive, plural *kotki*. Putting the number after the noun (*kotki dwa* instead of *dwa kotki*) is a poetic word order you often find in folk songs.
- *szaro-bure*: *szary* (grey) plus *bury* (dun, mousy brown), the classic tabby colouring.
- *obydwa* = both (for masculine and neuter nouns; with feminine nouns it is *obydwie*).
- *nic nie będą robiły*: Polish uses a double negative (*nic nie*, literally "nothing not"), and the future of an imperfective verb is built with *być* plus a past-tense form: *będą robiły* (they will be doing).

![Two tabby kittens sitting by a railing, like the "szaro-bure" pair from one of the best-loved Polish lullabies, Aaa, kotki dwa — Image by dimitrisvetsikas1969 from Pixabay](/blog/polish-lullabies-two-tabby-kittens.webp)

**How to use it:** like most Polish lullabies, sing it slowly while rocking, and stretch the "a-a-a" as long as your breath allows. With older toddlers, hold up two fingers on *kotki dwa*. It is the simplest possible way to practise the number two and the word *kot* at the same time.

## Lulajże, Jezuniu: A Christmas Carol That Doubles as a Lullaby

Not all Polish lullabies were written for an ordinary cradle. *Lulajże, Jezuniu* is a Christmas carol in which the singer hushes the baby Jesus and asks His Mother to comfort Him. Polish Wikipedia describes it as a pastorałka with the character of a lullaby, which is why it sits so naturally beside a cradle. It has been around since at least the early 18th century, and Chopin even quoted its tune in his first Scherzo. We cover its history and pronunciation in our guide to [Polish Christmas carols](/blog/polish-christmas-carols); here is the opening:

> Lulajże, Jezuniu, moja perełko,
> Lulaj, ulubione me pieścidełko.

**Meaning:** Hush now, little Jesus, my little pearl; hush, my dearly loved little darling.

**What it teaches:** the verb *lulać* (to lull to sleep) and its command form *lulaj*, softened with the particle *-że*. It is also a gentle lesson in the vocative, the case for calling someone: *Jezuniu*, *perełko* (from *perełka*, little pearl). Swap in your child's name and you have a ready-made Polish endearment.

## Wlazł kotek na płotek: The First Song Many Kids Play

*Wlazł kotek na płotek* ("A Kitten Climbed Onto the Fence") is a folk song from the 18th century, and its tune is so simple that it is often the first piece children pick out on a piano or recorder. Strictly speaking it is a folk song rather than a lullaby, but it is widely used as one, so it belongs on any list of Polish lullabies. According to [Polish Wikipedia's entry on the song](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wlaz%C5%82_kotek_na_p%C5%82otek), the folklorist Oskar Kolberg recorded these original words:

> Wlazł kotek na płotek i mruga,
> piękna to piosneczka niedługa.
> Wlazł kurek na murek i pieje;
> niech się nikt z tych piosnek nie śmieje.

**Meaning:** A kitten climbed onto the fence and blinks; it's a pretty little song, and not a long one. A little rooster climbed onto a wall and crows; let nobody laugh at these songs.

Like all traditional songs, it is sung with slightly different words from region to region, and a popular second verse goes: *Niedługa, niekrótka, a w sam raz. Zaśpiewaj, koteczku, jeszcze raz* ("Not long, not short, just right. Sing it, little kitten, one more time"). The composer Stanisław Moniuszko even wrote a three-voice canon on the tune in 1849.

**What it teaches:**
- Rhyming diminutives: *kotek / płotek*, *kurek / murek*. *Płot* is a fence, *mur* a wall, and *kurek* is an old word for a little rooster (in modern Polish *kurek* also means a tap).
- *wlazł*: the past tense of *wleźć*, a colloquial verb for climbing onto or into something.
- *na płotek*: after a verb of motion, *na* takes the accusative ("onto the fence").
- *mrugać* = to blink or wink. Blink at your child on *i mruga* and you have the gesture.

## Idzie rak, nieborak: The Tickle Rhyme

*Idzie rak, nieborak* ("Here Comes the Crayfish, Poor Thing") is a favourite among Polish nursery rhymes for babies, because it ends in a gentle pinch. It is a play rhyme listed among traditional contact games by Poland's Forum Muzyki Tradycyjnej, and the shortest version is just two lines:

> Idzie rak, nieborak,
> jak uszczypnie, będzie znak!

**Meaning:** Here comes the crayfish, the poor old thing; if it pinches you, it'll leave a mark!

A longer version adds a couplet: *Idzie rak, idzie rak, czasem naprzód, czasem wspak* ("Here comes the crayfish, sometimes forwards, sometimes backwards").

**The game:** walk two fingers slowly up the child's arm, leg or back while you chant, pausing to build suspense, and "pinch" (very softly) on *znak*. Some families make the crayfish go backwards on *wspak*, which children find hilarious.

**What it teaches:**
- *idzie* = is walking, from *iść*. It is one of the first motion verbs a child hears.
- *nieborak* = a poor thing, a creature you feel sorry for.
- *jak uszczypnie*: *jak* here means "if" or "when", and *uszczypnie* is a perfective verb with future meaning ("will pinch").
- *naprzód* (forward) and *wspak* (backwards). The crayfish's backwards walk is proverbial: *cofać się rakiem* ("to back away like a crayfish") means to retreat or back out.

## Tu sroczka kaszkę warzyła: The Magpie Finger Play

This is the classic Polish finger play, and the one researchers find most often. Where Polish lullabies soothe a baby to sleep, finger plays like this one wake a baby up. The magpie (*sroczka*) cooks porridge, burns her tail, and shares the food out among the child's fingers, all except the littlest. Versions vary a lot from family to family; this is a common one:

> Tu sroczka kaszkę warzyła,
> ogonek sobie sparzyła.
> Temu dała na miseczkę,
> temu dała na łyżeczkę,
> temu, bo grzecznie prosił,
> temu, bo wodę nosił,
> a temu najmniejszemu nic nie dała,
> tylko ogonkiem zamieszała
> i frr... daleko poleciała!

**Meaning:** Here the little magpie cooked some porridge and scalded her little tail. To this one she gave a bowlful, to this one a spoonful, to this one because he asked nicely, to this one because he carried water, but to this smallest one she gave nothing, she just stirred with her tail and whoosh, far away she flew!

![A magpie perched on a branch in Łódź, the "sroczka" of the Polish nursery rhyme Tu sroczka kaszkę warzyła — Photo: Jerzy Strzelecki, CC BY 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons](/blog/polish-lullabies-magpie.webp)

**The game:** many parents start by stirring a little circle in the child's palm on *tu* (here): that is the magpie's pot. Then you take each finger in turn as the magpie hands out food, starting from the thumb, so the littlest finger comes last. Folklorists describe the finale like this: the last finger is held as if you were pulling off its tip, and your hand rises into the air like the magpie flying away. In many homes it flies straight up the arm for a tickle.

**What it teaches:**
- The dative *temu* ("to this one") over and over, which is the easiest way to learn a dative ever invented.
- A whole kitchen of diminutives: *kaszka* (from *kasza*, porridge or groats), *ogonek* (from *ogon*, tail), *miseczka* (little bowl), *łyżeczka* (teaspoon).
- *warzyć* is an old verb for cooking or brewing; in the Dukla area researchers found the magpie brewing beer (*piwko*) instead of porridge, a reminder that Polish nursery rhymes always come in variants.

## Kosi, kosi, łapci: The Clapping Rhyme for Babcia

If Polish lullabies are for bedtime, clapping rhymes are for the daytime. This one is a *klaskanka* you can play with a baby who can sit up. You hold the child's hands and clap them together in rhythm. It is also known as *Koci, koci, łapci*, and the ending changes from region to region:

> Kosi, kosi, łapci,
> pojedziem do babci.
> Babcia da nam mleczka,
> a dziadek jajeczka.

**Meaning:** Clap, clap, little hands, we'll go and see Grandma. Grandma will give us some milk, and Grandpa a little egg.

The opening *kosi, kosi* is sound play rather than a real phrase, and *łapci* is a playful form related to *łapki* (little paws, a common baby word for hands). *Pojedziem* is an old, rhythmic short form of *pojedziemy* (we will go).

![A grandmother and a baby touching foreheads at home, the kind of moment Polish nursery rhymes like Kosi, kosi, łapci are made for — Photo by Helena Jankovičová Kováčová on Pexels](/blog/polish-lullabies-grandmother-baby.webp)

**What it teaches:** family words first of all: *babcia* (grandma) and *dziadek* (grandpa). Our guide to [family members in Polish](/blog/family-members-in-polish) goes much further. It also shows the genitive in its "some of" role: *mleczko*, the diminutive of *mleko* (milk), becomes *da nam mleczka* (will give us some milk). And *do babci* uses *do* plus the genitive to say "to Grandma's".

## Jedzie, jedzie pan: The Knee-Bouncing Rhyme

Nothing could be further from quiet Polish lullabies than *Jedzie, jedzie pan* ("Here Rides the Gentleman"), a *husianka*, a lap rhyme in which the child "rides" on your knees. The Forum Muzyki Tradycyjnej's [traditional play cards for families](https://muzykatradycyjna.pl/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/LaboratoriumZabawTradycyjnych2024_06_Zabawy-kontaktowe.pdf) give this version:

> Jedzie, jedzie pan, pan,
> na koniku sam, sam.
> Jedzie, jedzie chłop, chłop,
> na koniku hop, hop!
> Jedzie, jedzie baba
> i z konika spada!

**Meaning:** Here rides the gentleman, all alone on his little horse. Here rides the farmer, on his little horse, bump, bump! Here rides the old woman, and she falls off her little horse!

**The game:** sit the child on your knees facing you. Bounce gently and smoothly for the elegant *pan*, bumpily for the *chłop*, and on *spada* open your knees so the child drops a little, while you hold them securely. Expect to repeat it until your legs give out.

**What it teaches:** the verb *jechać* (to ride, to travel by vehicle or on horseback) in the form *jedzie*, the diminutive *konik* (little horse, from *koń*) in two cases (*na koniku*, *z konika*), and *spadać* (to fall off). *Baba* is an old word for a country woman; in modern Polish it can sound rude, but in the rhyme it is pure folk fun.

## Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi: The Circle Game

*Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi* ("The Old Bear Is Fast Asleep") is the loudest entry here and not one of the Polish lullabies at all: it is a running game for a group of children, a Polish cousin of "What's the Time, Mr Wolf?". It was already described in a 1923 handbook for teachers by Maria Germanówna, and its tune comes from the folk song *Od Krakowa ciemny las*.

> Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi,
> stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi.
> My się go boimy, na palcach chodzimy,
> jak się zbudzi, to nas zje,
> jak się zbudzi, to nas zje.
> Pierwsza godzina, niedźwiedź śpi,
> druga godzina, niedźwiedź chrapie,
> trzecia godzina, niedźwiedź łapie!

**Meaning:** The old bear is fast asleep. We're scared of him, so we tiptoe around; if he wakes up, he'll eat us! One o'clock, the bear is sleeping; two o'clock, the bear is snoring; three o'clock, the bear is catching!

**The game:** one child is the bear and crouches in the middle, pretending to sleep. The others walk around in a circle singing, crouching down on the repeated lines. On *łapie!* everyone scatters and the bear gives chase; whoever is caught first becomes the next bear.

**What it teaches:**
- *bać się* (to be afraid) takes the genitive: *my się go boimy* (we are afraid of him).
- *na palcach* = on tiptoe (literally "on fingers/toes"; Polish uses *palce* for both).
- Ordinal numbers with *godzina* (hour): *pierwsza*, *druga*, *trzecia*, which children can later reuse for telling the time.
- Three lively verbs: *śpi* (sleeps), *chrapie* (snores), *łapie* (catches).

## Scenario: Bedtime in a Bilingual Home

Here is how Polish lullabies slot into an ordinary evening. Mum speaks Polish at home; her four-year-old answers in a mix of both languages but knows the bedtime script, and the Polish lullabies that go with it, by heart.

- **Mama:** Pora spać, kochanie. *(Time to sleep, sweetheart.)*
- **Dziecko:** Jeszcze jedną piosenkę! *(One more song!)*
- **Mama:** Dobrze, ale tylko jedną. Którą chcesz? *(OK, but just one. Which one do you want?)*
- **Dziecko:** O kotkach! *(The one about the kittens!)*
- **Mama:** A-a-a, kotki dwa... Zamknij oczka. *(Hush, two kittens... Close your little eyes.)*
- **Dziecko:** A jutro „Idzie rak"? *(And tomorrow "Idzie rak"?)*
- **Mama:** Jutro. Dobranoc, śpij dobrze. *(Tomorrow. Good night, sleep well.)*

Notice how much grammar a child gets for free here: accusative *jedną piosenkę*, locative *o kotkach*, the diminutive *oczka* (from *oczy*, eyes) and the imperatives *zamknij* and *śpij*.

## Tips for Using Polish Lullabies With Kids Learning Polish

Polish lullabies and nursery rhymes work best as part of daily life, not as lessons. A few habits make a real difference.

### Tie Each Rhyme to a Routine

Keep Polish lullabies like *Aaa, kotki dwa* for bedtime, play *Kosi, kosi, łapci* during nappy changes, do *Jedzie, jedzie pan* after lunch. When a rhyme belongs to a moment, children start asking for it themselves, and asking is the first step to saying it.

### Let the Gestures Carry the Meaning

You rarely need to translate Polish lullabies or rhymes. The pinch in *Idzie rak* explains *uszczypnie* better than any English word, and the magpie flying off explains *poleciała*. Keep the song in Polish and let the action do the work.

### Repeat Without Apology

Small children love hearing the same Polish lullabies fifty times, and every repetition is practice. Resist the urge to "move on" to new material. Five well-loved Polish lullabies used every day beat twenty songs sung once.

### Hand Over the Last Word

Once one of your Polish lullabies or rhymes is familiar, pause before the final word and let the child fill it in: *jak uszczypnie, będzie...* "znak!" It is the gentlest possible speaking practice, and it turns listening into talking.

### Accept the Variants

Grandma may sing *Wlazł kotek* differently from the version in a book, and both are right. Polish lullabies and rhymes have always lived in many versions; pick the one your family uses and keep it consistent for your child.

### Learn Them Yourself First

If you are a learner rather than a native speaker, Polish lullabies and rhymes are an excellent beginner workout too. Say each line slowly, check the stress on the second-to-last syllable, and record yourself. By the time you have learned three Polish lullabies, you will have practised most of the sounds of the language.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most famous Polish lullaby?

*Aaa, kotki dwa* is probably the best known of all Polish lullabies. Almost every Polish adult can sing the first lines about the two grey kittens who will do nothing but play with the children.

### What does "kotki dwa" mean?

*Kotki dwa* means "two kittens" (or "two little cats"). *Kotki* is the plural of *kotek*, the diminutive of *kot* (cat), and the number is placed after the noun for rhythm, as often happens in folk songs.

### What are the best Polish nursery rhymes for babies?

For babies, choose rhymes with touch and movement: *Tu sroczka kaszkę warzyła* (finger play), *Idzie rak, nieborak* (tickle), *Kosi, kosi, łapci* (clapping) and *Jedzie, jedzie pan* (knee bouncing). For groups of older children, *Stary niedźwiedź mocno śpi* is a favourite.

### Are traditional Polish lullabies free to use?

Traditional folk lullabies and rhymes like the ones in this guide have no known author and are shared freely. Modern songs and poems by named authors, and modern recordings or arrangements of old songs, can still be under copyright, so check before you print or record them.

### Where can I find recordings of Polish lullabies?

Search for the song title together with the word *kołysanka* (lullaby) or *piosenka dla dzieci* (children's song) and you will find versions by choirs, folk ensembles and children's performers. Albums of Polish lullabies are also common in Polish bookshops, often sold together with a songbook.

### Can I use Polish lullabies if my own Polish isn't perfect?

Yes. Children care about your voice and attention far more than your accent when you sing Polish lullabies, and singing together is one of the easiest ways for parents to keep Polish in the home. Learning the rhymes alongside your child can even improve your own pronunciation.

Polish lullabies are small, but they carry a lot: family history, the rhythm of the language and a stack of grammar that children pick up without noticing. Start with one rhyme tonight, add a gesture, and repeat it until your child sings the last word for you.
