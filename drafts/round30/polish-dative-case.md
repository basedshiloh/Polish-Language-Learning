The Polish dative case is the case of the receiver: the person you give something to, buy something for, help, thank or trust. In Polish it is called **celownik**, from *cel*, meaning "target" or "aim", and that name is a good way to remember it. Whenever an action is aimed at someone, that someone usually lands in the dative.

You meet it less often than the accusative or the genitive, but you meet it in some of the most common phrases in the language. *Podoba mi się* (I like it), *zimno mi* (I'm cold), *dziękuję ci* (thank you) and *jak ci na imię?* (what's your name?) are all dative. If you have ever wondered why it is *mi* and not *ja*, this guide is the answer.

Below you will find the two dative questions, the verbs that trigger the case (including a few that surprise English speakers), the impersonal "feeling" phrases, every set of Polish dative case endings with the tricky exceptions, adjectives, pronouns with their long and short forms, the four dative prepositions, and a practice section with answers. If you want to see where the dative sits next to the other six cases first, our guide on [when to use each Polish case](/blog/when-to-use-polish-cases) gives the big picture.

## What Is the Polish Dative Case (Celownik)?

Every Polish case answers a pair of questions. The Polish dative case answers **komu?** (to whom? for whom?) and **czemu?** (to what?). In practice you will use *komu?* far more, because the receiver of an action is nearly always a person or an animal.

| Case | Polish name | Questions | Typical job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | mianownik | kto? co? | the subject |
| Accusative | biernik | kogo? co? | the direct object |
| Genitive | dopełniacz | kogo? czego? | negation, possession, quantity |
| **Dative** | **celownik** | **komu? czemu?** | **the receiver, the indirect object** |
| Instrumental | narzędnik | kim? czym? | "with", professions after być |
| Locative | miejscownik | o kim? o czym? | location, after w, na, o, przy, po |
| Vocative | wołacz | (none) | addressing someone directly |

A quick test: ask *Komu?* about your sentence. *Daję prezent. Komu? Mamie.* (I'm giving a present. To whom? To Mum.) The answer goes into the dative.

One small warning about *czemu?*. In everyday speech, *czemu?* also simply means "why?", as a casual alternative to *dlaczego?*. *Czemu nie?* means "Why not?", not "To what not?". Context makes the difference obvious.

## The Indirect Object: Giving, Buying, Helping, Thanking

The core job of the Polish dative case is the indirect object. In English we mark it with "to" or "for", or with word order ("I gave **my brother** a book"). Polish marks it with an ending instead, so the word order is free.

A typical sentence has two objects: the thing (accusative) and the receiver (dative).

| Polish | English | Receiver (dative) | Thing (accusative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daję bratu książkę. | I'm giving my brother a book. | bratu | książkę |
| Kupuję mamie kwiaty. | I'm buying Mum flowers. | mamie | kwiaty |
| Pokazuję koledze zdjęcia. | I'm showing my friend photos. | koledze | zdjęcia |
| Czytam dzieciom bajkę. | I'm reading the children a story. | dzieciom | bajkę |
| Wysyłam szefowi e-mail. | I'm sending my boss an email. | szefowi | e-mail |

The most common verbs of this type, with their aspect pairs (imperfective / perfective):

- **dawać / dać** (to give): *Dam ci znać.* (I'll let you know.)
- **kupować / kupić** (to buy): *Kupię ci kawę.* (I'll buy you a coffee.)
- **mówić / powiedzieć** (to tell, say): *Powiedz mi prawdę.* (Tell me the truth.)
- **pokazywać / pokazać** (to show)
- **wysyłać / wysłać** (to send)
- **pożyczać / pożyczyć** (to lend): *Pożyczysz mi długopis?* (Will you lend me a pen?)
- **obiecywać / obiecać** (to promise)

### Dziękować and pomagać: dative with only one object

Two very common verbs take the dative even though there is no "thing" being handed over.

**Dziękować / podziękować** (to thank) puts the person in the dative and the reason after *za* + accusative: *Dziękuję ci za pomoc.* (Thank you for the help.) *Dziękuję panu.* (Thank you, sir.) English treats "you" as a direct object here, Polish does not.

**Pomagać / pomóc** (to help) also takes the dative: *Pomagam mamie w kuchni.* (I help Mum in the kitchen.) *Pomóż mi!* (Help me!) Learners often say *pomóż mnie* or *pomagam mamę*, which sound wrong to Polish ears.

## Verbs That Take the Polish Dative Case Unexpectedly

Some verbs take the Polish dative case where English uses a plain direct object. These are worth learning as fixed pairs, verb + *komu?*, because your English instincts will push you toward the accusative.

| Verb (impf. / pf.) | Meaning | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| pomagać / pomóc | to help | Pomagam bratu. | I help my brother. |
| ufać / zaufać | to trust | Ufam mojemu lekarzowi. | I trust my doctor. |
| wierzyć / uwierzyć | to believe (someone) | Nie wierzę mu. | I don't believe him. |
| przyglądać się / przyjrzeć się | to look closely at | Przyglądam się mapie. | I'm studying the map. |
| przeszkadzać / przeszkodzić | to disturb, bother | Nie przeszkadzaj mi! | Don't disturb me! |
| pozwalać / pozwolić | to allow | Mama pozwala dzieciom grać. | Mum lets the children play. |
| radzić / poradzić | to advise | Radzę ci odpocząć. | I advise you to rest. |
| kibicować | to cheer for, support | Kibicuję Polsce. | I'm rooting for Poland. |
| odpowiadać / odpowiedzieć | to reply to | Odpowiadam klientowi. | I'm replying to the customer. |

Note the split with *wierzyć*: **wierzyć komuś** means "to believe someone" (dative), but **wierzyć w coś** means "to believe in something" and takes *w* + accusative: *Wierzę w ciebie.* (I believe in you.)

A few verbs combine the dative with the genitive. **Życzyć** (to wish) is the one you will use most: *Życzę ci szczęścia!* (I wish you happiness!) *Życzę wam wesołych świąt!* (I wish you all a merry Christmas!) The person is dative, the thing wished is genitive. **Gratulować** (to congratulate) works the same way: *Gratuluję ci sukcesu.*

## Impersonal Feelings: Zimno Mi, Podoba Mi Się, Chce Mi Się

This is the part of the Polish dative case that beginners meet first, often without realising it. Polish describes many physical and emotional states impersonally: instead of "I am cold", it says something closer to "it is cold **to me**". The person who feels it goes into the dative.

### Feeling word + dative: how you feel

| Polish | Literal | Natural English |
|---|---|---|
| Zimno mi. | cold to me | I'm cold. |
| Gorąco mi. | hot to me | I'm hot. |
| Smutno mi. | sad to me | I'm sad. |
| Przykro mi. | sorry to me | I'm sorry. |
| Wstyd mi. | shame to me | I'm ashamed. |
| Niedobrze mi. | not-good to me | I feel sick. |
| Wszystko mi jedno. | all to me one | I don't mind either way. |

You can add *jest* (*Jest mi zimno*) and the meaning stays the same. Change the pronoun and the "feeler" changes: *Zimno ci?* (Are you cold?), *Smutno jej.* (She's sad.), *Gorąco nam.* (We're hot.)

![A person bundled up in a fur-lined hood and a thick wool scarf in the snow, the moment for the Polish dative case phrase zimno mi (I'm cold) — Photo by Brandon Benedict on Pexels](/blog/polish-dative-case-cold.webp)

### Podobać się: "it pleases me"

*Podobać się* is how Polish says "to like" something you see or experience. Grammatically, the thing you like is the subject, and you are the dative receiver of the pleasure:

- *Podoba mi się ten film.* (I like this film. Literally: this film pleases me.)
- *Podobają mi się te buty.* (I like these shoes.) The verb is plural because *buty* is plural.
- *Czy podoba ci się Kraków?* (Do you like Kraków?)

### Chce mi się, wydaje mi się and friends

A small group of verbs work the same way. They appear in the third-person singular, and the person goes into the dative:

- **Chce mi się pić.** (I'm thirsty. Literally: it wants itself to me to drink.)
- **Chce mi się spać.** (I'm sleepy.)
- **Nie chce mi się.** (I can't be bothered.) One of the most useful phrases in colloquial Polish.
- **Wydaje mi się, że…** (It seems to me that…)
- **Udało mi się!** (I managed it! / I did it!)
- **Smakuje ci?** (Do you like the taste? Is it good?)
- **Brakuje mi ciebie.** (I miss you. Literally: you are lacking to me.)

And the question every learner hears in their first lesson, *Jak ci na imię?* (What's your name?), is a dative construction too: "how is the name to you?"

## Polish Dative Case Endings: Singular Nouns

Here are the Polish dative case endings for singular nouns. The masculine and neuter patterns are short. The feminine one needs more attention because of consonant changes.

| Gender | Ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine (most nouns) | **-owi** | student → studentowi, Adam → Adamowi, nauczyciel → nauczycielowi, Tomek → Tomkowi |
| Masculine (short list) | **-u** | brat → bratu, kot → kotu, pies → psu |
| Feminine (hard stem) | **-e** + consonant change | mama → mamie, siostra → siostrze, córka → córce |
| Feminine (soft stem, -i, consonant) | **-i / -y** | babcia → babci, pani → pani, noc → nocy, ulica → ulicy |
| Neuter | **-u** | dziecko → dziecku, okno → oknu, morze → morzu |

### Masculine: -owi, and the -u exceptions

Almost every masculine noun takes **-owi** in the Polish dative case: *studentowi, lekarzowi, szefowi, klientowi, domowi*. If the noun has a mobile *e* in the nominative, it drops out: *Tomek → Tomkowi*.

A short list of common masculine nouns takes **-u** instead. Learn these as a set, because they are among the most frequent nouns in the language:

| Nominative | Dative | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| brat | bratu | brother |
| ojciec | ojcu | father |
| pan | panu | sir, Mr, gentleman |
| kot | kotu | cat |
| pies | psu | dog |
| Bóg | Bogu | God |
| chłopiec | chłopcu | boy |
| świat | światu | world |

You will hear *Dziękuję panu* in every Polish shop, and *dzięki Bogu* (thank God) in everyday speech. Note the vowel changes: *pies → psu*, *ojciec → ojcu*, *Bóg → Bogu*.

Masculine nouns that end in **-a**, such as *tata*, *kolega* or *mężczyzna*, decline like feminine nouns in the singular: *tacie, koledze, mężczyźnie*. They are still masculine for adjectives: *mojemu koledze*.

### Feminine: -e with alternations, or -i/-y

Hard-stem feminine nouns take **-e**, and the final consonant softens. The good news: these feminine Polish dative case endings are identical to the feminine locative, so if you know *w Polsce* (in Poland), you already know *Polsce*. Our guide to the [Polish locative case](/blog/polish-locative-case) drills the same alternations from the other side.

| Stem ends in | Change | Example |
|---|---|---|
| -m, -n, -w, -b, -p | + ie | mama → mamie, żona → żonie, Ewa → Ewie |
| -t | t → ci | kobieta → kobiecie |
| -d | d → dzi | woda → wodzie, Magda → Magdzie |
| -r | r → rz | siostra → siostrze |
| -ł | ł → l | szkoła → szkole |
| -k | k → c | córka → córce, Polska → Polsce |
| -g | g → dz | droga → drodze, noga → nodze |
| -ch | ch → sz | mucha → musze |

Soft-stem nouns, nouns in -i, and feminine nouns ending in a consonant take **-i** (or **-y** after c, cz, sz, rz, ż): *babcia → babci, ciocia → cioci, Kasia → Kasi, lekcja → lekcji, pani → pani, noc → nocy, miłość → miłości, ulica → ulicy, praca → pracy*.

### Neuter: -u

Neuter nouns are the easy group in the Polish dative case. They take **-u**: *dziecko → dziecku, okno → oknu, miasto → miastu, morze → morzu, słońce → słońcu*. Nouns in -ę add a stem extension: *imię → imieniu, zwierzę → zwierzęciu*. Nouns in -um, such as *muzeum*, do not change in the singular.

## Polish Dative Case Plural: -om for Everything

The plural is the best news in this whole article. In the Polish dative case plural, every gender takes **-om**.

| Nominative plural | Dative plural |
|---|---|
| studenci | studentom |
| kobiety | kobietom |
| dzieci | dzieciom |
| koty | kotom |
| rodzice | rodzicom |
| ludzie | ludziom |
| bracia | braciom |
| przyjaciele | przyjaciołom |

Irregular plurals keep their irregular stem but still end in -om: *ludziom, dzieciom, braciom, przyjaciołom*. Even body parts with unusual plurals follow the rule: *oczom* (eyes), *uszom* (ears), *rękom* (hands).

## Adjectives, Possessives and "This" in the Polish Dative Case

Adjectives agree with their noun in the dative, as in every other case. The Polish dative case gives you just three adjective endings to learn.

| | Masculine / Neuter | Feminine | Plural (all) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Adjective ending | **-emu** (-iemu after k, g) | **-ej** (-iej after k, g) | **-ym** (-im after k, g) |
| dobry | dobremu | dobrej | dobrym |
| polski | polskiemu | polskiej | polskim |
| ten / ta / te | temu | tej | tym |
| mój / moja / moje | mojemu | mojej | moim |
| nasz / nasza / nasze | naszemu | naszej | naszym |

Examples in context:

- *Daję prezent mojemu młodszemu bratu.* (I'm giving a present to my younger brother.)
- *Pomagam starszej sąsiadce.* (I help my elderly neighbour.)
- *Dziękuję naszym nauczycielom.* (I thank our teachers.)
- *Ufam temu lekarzowi.* (I trust this doctor.)

The possessives *jego* (his), *jej* (her) and *ich* (their) never change: *Daję prezent jej mamie.* (I'm giving a present to her mum.)

## Polish Dative Case Pronouns: Mnie or Mi? Tobie or Ci?

Dative pronouns are where the Polish dative case becomes really practical. Most singular pronouns have two forms: a **long, stressed** form and a **short, unstressed** one.

| Nominative | Long (stressed) | Short (unstressed) | After a preposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| ja | mnie | mi | mnie |
| ty | tobie | ci | tobie |
| on / ono | jemu | mu | niemu |
| ona | jej | jej | niej |
| my | nam | nam | nam |
| wy | wam | wam | wam |
| oni / one | im | im | nim |
| pan / pani | panu / pani | (no short form) | panu / pani |

### When to use the short form

The **short forms** *mi, ci, mu* are the default. Use them in neutral sentences where the pronoun is not the focus, and never put them at the very start of a sentence:

- *Daj mi spokój.* (Leave me alone.)
- *Dam ci znać.* (I'll let you know.)
- *Powiedz mu, że dzwoniłam.* (Tell him I called.)
- *Podoba mi się.* (I like it.)

### When to use the long form

Switch to the **long forms** *mnie, tobie, jemu* in three situations:

1. **At the start of a sentence:** *Mnie się to podoba.* (I like it, as for me.)
2. **For emphasis or contrast:** *Daj to jemu, nie mnie!* (Give it to him, not to me!) *Mnie jest zimno, a tobie?* (I'm cold, and you?)
3. **After a preposition:** *dzięki tobie* (thanks to you), *przeciwko mnie* (against me). For third-person pronouns, the preposition also adds an n-: *dzięki niemu, dzięki niej, dzięki nim*.

*Jej, nam, wam, im* have no separate short form, so the long-or-short question never comes up (though *jej* and *im* still switch to *niej* and *nim* after a preposition). To see these pronouns side by side with their accusative, genitive and instrumental forms, keep the site's pronoun chart open:

## Prepositions That Take the Polish Dative Case

Only four common prepositions take the Polish dative case, which makes this section quick. Most Polish prepositions take the genitive, accusative, instrumental or locative, and our guide to [Polish prepositions and the cases they take](/blog/polish-prepositions) covers those.

| Preposition | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **dzięki** | thanks to | dzięki tobie (thanks to you), dzięki Bogu (thank God) |
| **przeciw / przeciwko** | against | przeciwko wojnie (against war), Nie mam nic przeciwko temu. (I have nothing against it.) |
| **ku** | towards (formal, literary) | ku morzu (towards the sea), ku mojemu zdziwieniu (to my surprise) |
| **wbrew** | contrary to, despite | wbrew pozorom (contrary to appearances) |

*Dzięki* is the one you will use daily. On its own, *dzięki!* is a casual "thanks!", and with a noun it means "thanks to": *Dzięki tobie zdałem egzamin.* (Thanks to you, I passed the exam.)

*Ku* sounds old-fashioned in most contexts, but it survives in fixed phrases such as *ku mojemu zdziwieniu* and on memorial plaques (*ku pamięci*, "in memory of"). *Wbrew pozorom* is a handy set phrase for spoken Polish.

## Scenario: Buying a Birthday Present

Here is a short dialogue between two friends shopping for a gift. Every Polish dative case form is in bold.

> **Ola:** Co kupujesz **mamie** na urodziny?
> *What are you buying Mum for her birthday?*
>
> **Kuba:** Nie wiem. Wydaje **mi** się, że książkę. Ona lubi kryminały.
> *I don't know. I think a book. She likes crime novels.*
>
> **Ola:** Podoba **ci** się ta?
> *Do you like this one?*
>
> **Kuba:** Tak, ale **mojej mamie** chyba nie. A co dajesz **swojemu bratu**?
> *Yes, but I don't think my mum would. And what are you giving your brother?*
>
> **Ola:** Nic. Nie chce **mi** się szukać. Kupię **mu** kawę i wystarczy.
> *Nothing. I can't be bothered to look. I'll buy him a coffee and that'll do.*
>
> **Kuba:** Dzięki **tobie** mam pomysł: kupię **mamie** kwiaty!
> *Thanks to you I have an idea: I'll buy Mum flowers!*

Notice *swojemu bratu*. When the receiver belongs to the subject of the sentence, Polish uses the reflexive possessive *swój*, which also declines: *swojemu, swojej, swoim*.

## Common Mistakes With the Polish Dative Case

These are the Polish dative case errors teachers hear most often from English-speaking learners:

1. **Accusative after pomagać:** *pomagam mamę* ✗ → *pomagam mamie* ✓
2. **Nominative in feelings:** *ja jestem zimno* ✗ → *zimno mi* or *jest mi zimno* ✓
3. **Short form at the start:** *Mi się podoba* ✗ (very common in casual speech, but style guides recommend the long form at the start) → *Mnie się podoba* or *Podoba mi się* ✓
4. **Long form with no emphasis:** *Daj mnie to* puts heavy stress on "me" → *Daj mi to* ✓
5. **Forgetting the -u group:** *bratowi, kotowi* ✗ → *bratu, kotu* ✓
6. **Missing the consonant change:** *córkie, siostrie* ✗ → *córce, siostrze* ✓
7. **Using jemu after a preposition:** *dzięki jemu* ✗ → *dzięki niemu* ✓

If mistakes like these feel familiar, they are part of the learning process. The dative tends to click once you stop translating "to me" word for word and start thinking "who is the target?".

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the Polish dative case called?

It is called **celownik**. The name is linked to *cel* (target, aim), which matches its job: it marks the person or thing an action is aimed at. Its questions are *komu?* and *czemu?*.

### What are the Polish dative case endings?

In the singular: masculine **-owi** (with a short list taking **-u**, such as *bratu, ojcu, panu, kotu, psu*), feminine **-e** with a consonant change or **-i/-y**, and neuter **-u**. In the plural, every gender takes **-om**. Adjectives take **-emu** (m/n), **-ej** (f) and **-ym/-im** (plural).

### What is the difference between mi and mnie?

Both are Polish dative case forms of *ja*. **Mi** is the short, unstressed form you use by default (*Podoba mi się*). **Mnie** is the long form for the start of a sentence, for emphasis, or after a preposition (*Mnie się podoba*, *dzięki mnie*).

### Is the Polish dative case the same as the locative?

Not in general, but the **feminine singular** dative and locative are identical: *mamie, siostrze, córce, Polsce*. Masculine and neuter forms differ (*bratu* vs *o bracie*), and the plural differs too (*kotom* vs *o kotach*).

### Why is it "zimno mi" and not "jestem zimny"?

*Jestem zimny* would describe you as a cold object (or a cold person in character). Feeling cold is expressed impersonally with the Polish dative case: *zimno mi*, "it is cold to me". The same pattern gives you *smutno mi, przykro mi* and *gorąco mi*.

### Which verbs take the Polish dative case?

The most common ones are *dawać/dać, kupować/kupić, mówić/powiedzieć, pomagać/pomóc, dziękować, ufać, wierzyć* (someone), *przeszkadzać, pozwalać, życzyć, przyglądać się* and *podobać się*. Learn each as "verb + komu?".

The Polish dative case looks intimidating in a table, but in real conversation it shows up in a fairly small set of patterns: giving and buying, helping and thanking, feelings, and four prepositions. Learn *mi, ci, mu*, the -om plural and the handful of -u nouns, and you will already handle most of the dative you hear. For a scholarly overview of how all seven cases fit together, the [Polish grammar article on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_grammar) is a solid reference to keep alongside this guide.
