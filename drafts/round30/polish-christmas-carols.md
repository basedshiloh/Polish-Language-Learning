Polish Christmas carols, called *kolędy*, are the soundtrack of a Polish winter. They fill churches at the midnight Mass, drift out of kitchens while the last pierogi are being folded, and keep going long after the tree has lost its first needles. Some of them are more than three hundred years old, and most Polish families can sing at least the first verse of a dozen without opening a songbook.

For a learner, that makes Polish Christmas carols a gift. The texts are short, rhythmic and repetitive, the vocabulary is concrete (manger, shepherds, star, hay), and the grammar hides in plain sight: vocatives, diminutives, imperatives and a few lovely archaic verb forms. Sing a carol ten times and you have practised Polish pronunciation more than most drills would manage.

This guide walks through nine essential kolędy. For each one you get the history in brief, the first verse in Polish (traditional, public-domain texts), our own plain-English rendering of what it means, and a handful of vocabulary and pronunciation notes. Then we look at the customs around them: the carolers with their star, the midnight Mass, and why Poles keep singing until 2 February.

## What Are Kolędy? Polish Christmas Carols Explained

The word *kolęda* comes from the Latin *calendae*, the first day of a Roman month, and it originally meant more than a song. In Polish folk custom it referred to New Year wishes, to the small gifts given to carolers, and to the visit itself. Groups of young people would go "po kolędzie" (on a carol round), sing at each house and receive food in return. Over time the word settled on its main modern meaning: a religious song about the birth of Christ.

Today Polish Christmas carols are sung in three main places. In church, from the midnight Mass onwards. At home, especially at the end of the Christmas Eve supper. And on the street or doorstep, where *kolędnicy* (carolers) still make the rounds in many villages. That mix of liturgy, family and folk theatre is what makes the Polish tradition so rich.

### Kolęda vs Pastorałka

You will often see the phrase *kolędy i pastorałki* on album covers and concert posters. The two overlap, but they are not identical.

- **Kolęda**: a Christmas song with religious content, the kind that is sung during Mass. *Bóg się rodzi* and *Wśród nocnej ciszy* are classic kolędy.
- **Pastorałka**: from Latin *pastoralis*, "of shepherds". A cheerful folk song about the Nativity, often told from the shepherds' point of view, with humour, dialect and everyday rural detail. Pastorałki were traditionally sung at home and by carolers rather than in the liturgy.

The line is blurry. Polish Wikipedia even lists *Lulajże, Jezuniu*, one of the most beloved Polish Christmas carols, as a pastorałka because of its lullaby character. In everyday speech most Poles simply call all of them kolędy.

## Bóg się rodzi: The Queen of Polish Christmas Carols

*Bóg się rodzi* ("God Is Born") is often called the queen of Polish Christmas carols. The text is by the Enlightenment poet **Franciszek Karpiński**, who wrote it as *Pieśń o Narodzeniu Pańskim* (Song of the Lord's Nativity). It was published in 1792 in his *Pieśni nabożne* (Devotional Songs), printed in Supraśl, and tradition says it was first sung at Christmas that year in Białystok. The stately melody moves in the rhythm of a polonaise; its origin is uncertain.

The poem is built on paradoxes, which is why it sounds grander than most Polish Christmas carols:

> Bóg się rodzi, moc truchleje,
> Pan niebiosów obnażony,
> Ogień krzepnie, blask ciemnieje,
> Ma granice nieskończony.
> Wzgardzony, okryty chwałą,
> Śmiertelny, Król nad wiekami;
> A Słowo Ciałem się stało
> I mieszkało między nami.

**Meaning:** God is born and power trembles; the Lord of the heavens lies bare. Fire turns solid, brightness grows dark, the Infinite takes on limits. Scorned yet covered in glory, mortal yet King for all ages. And the Word became flesh and lived among us.

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *rodzić się* = to be born. *Bóg się rodzi* is literally "God is being born". Say *Bóg* as "book": a final consonant loses its voice in Polish.
- *truchleć* = to tremble with awe or fear; *blask* = glow, brilliance; *chwała* = glory.
- *Słowo Ciałem się stało*: after *stać się* (to become), Polish uses the instrumental case, so *ciało* (body, flesh) becomes *ciałem*. A perfect real-life example of the instrumental you meet in grammar books.

## Wśród nocnej ciszy: The Shepherds' Wake-Up Call

*Wśród nocnej ciszy* ("Amid the Silence of the Night") dates from around the turn of the 18th and 19th centuries; its author is unknown. It was already widely sung in the early 1800s and was printed in 1853 in a supplement to Father Michał Mioduszewski's church songbook. Many parishes open the midnight Mass with it, so of all Polish Christmas carols this is the one that sounds most like Christmas beginning.

> Wśród nocnej ciszy głos się rozchodzi:
> Wstańcie, pasterze, Bóg się wam rodzi!
> Czym prędzej się wybierajcie,
> Do Betlejem pospieszajcie
> Przywitać Pana.

**Meaning:** In the stillness of the night a voice spreads far and wide: "Get up, shepherds, God is born for you! Set out as fast as you can, hurry to Bethlehem to greet the Lord."

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *wśród* + genitive = among, amid. *Nocna cisza* (night silence) becomes *nocnej ciszy*. Pronounce *wśród* roughly as "fshroot": the *w* turns into *f* next to *ś*, and the final *d* is devoiced.
- *wstańcie* = get up (plural command); *pasterze* = shepherds (singular *pasterz*).
- *czym prędzej* = as quickly as possible, a handy fixed phrase outside carols too.
- *Bóg się wam rodzi*: *wam* is the dative "for you (all)".

## Lulajże, Jezuniu: The Lullaby Carol Chopin Quoted

*Lulajże, Jezuniu* ("Hush, Little Jesus") is the tender heart of the Polish Christmas carols repertoire. It probably dates from the second half of the 17th century; the earliest surviving copy of the text comes from a collection dated 1705, and a melody close to today's version was printed in 1843 in Mioduszewski's anthology of pastorałki and kolędy.

> Lulajże, Jezuniu, moja perełko,
> Lulaj, ulubione me pieścidełko.
> Lulajże, Jezuniu, lulajże, lulaj,
> A Ty Go, Matulu, w płaczu utulaj.

**Meaning:** Hush now, little Jesus, my little pearl; hush, my dearly loved little darling. Hush now, little Jesus, hush, hush; and you, dear Mother, comfort Him when He cries.

This carol has a famous concert-hall life. In the slower middle section of his **Scherzo No. 1 in B minor, Op. 20**, written in the early 1830s while he was stuck in Vienna and anxious about the November Uprising back home, Chopin quotes the melody of *Lulajże, Jezuniu*. A homesick Pole slipping one of the gentlest Polish Christmas carols into a stormy piano piece is a moment worth hearing; our profile of [Frederic Chopin and his Polish roots](/blog/frederic-chopin-poland) tells the rest of that story.

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *lulać* = to lull to sleep; *lulaj* is the command form, and the particle *-że* adds warmth and insistence: *lulajże*.
- *Jezuniu*, *perełko*, *Matulu* are all vocatives, the case used to address someone directly. *Jezunio*, *perełka* (little pearl, from *perła*) and *Matula* are affectionate diminutive forms.
- *pieścidełko* = little darling, from *pieścić* (to caress). Stress it on *-DEŁ-*: "pyesh-chee-DEW-ko", with *ł* sounding like English *w*.

## W żłobie leży: The Carol That Calls Everyone to the Manger

Among the older Polish Christmas carols, *W żłobie leży* ("He Lies in a Manger") dates from the 17th or 18th century. Jesuit tradition attributes the words to Piotr Skarga, the famous royal preacher, though this is not certain. Its proud, dance-like rhythm has led some writers to link the tune to a court polonaise.

> W żłobie leży, któż pobieży
> Kolędować Małemu
> Jezusowi Chrystusowi,
> Dziś do nas zesłanemu?
> Pastuszkowie, przybywajcie,
> Jemu wdzięcznie przygrywajcie,
> Jako Panu naszemu.

**Meaning:** He lies in a manger. Who will run to sing carols to little Jesus Christ, sent down to us today? Come along, shepherds, and play Him something lovely, as you would for our Lord.

The manger (*żłób*, diminutive *żłóbek*) is the centre of every Polish nativity scene, from tiny home cribs to the glittering *szopki* of Kraków; if you are curious about those, see our guide to the [Kraków nativity scene tradition](/blog/krakow-nativity-scene).

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *w żłobie*: locative of *żłób* (manger). Notice how *ó* changes to *o* in the ending. The cluster *w żł-* is pronounced "vzhw-" in one breath.
- *kolędować* = to go caroling, to sing kolędy.
- *Jezusowi Chrystusowi*, *Panu naszemu*: dative case ("to Jesus Christ", "to our Lord").
- *pastuszkowie* = little shepherds; the *-owie* plural is reserved for male persons.

## Cicha noc: The Polish Version of Silent Night

*Cicha noc* is the Polish version of *Stille Nacht*, the one imported song that sits completely at home among Polish Christmas carols. The original German words were written by Joseph Mohr and set to music by Franz Xaver Gruber, and the song was first performed at the midnight Mass of Christmas Eve 1818 in Oberndorf near Salzburg. The Polish text is not a word-for-word translation. The best-known version circulates without a clear author, and Polish Wikipedia notes that one Polish version was written around 1930 by Piotr Maszyński.

> Cicha noc, święta noc,
> Pokój niesie ludziom wszem,
> A u żłóbka Matka Święta
> Czuwa sama uśmiechnięta
> Nad Dzieciątka snem,
> Nad Dzieciątka snem.

**Meaning:** Silent night, holy night, bringing peace to every person. And by the little manger the Holy Mother keeps watch alone, smiling, over the Child's sleep.

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *cicha* = quiet, silent (*cisza* = silence). *Noc* is pronounced "nots": Polish *c* is always "ts".
- *wszem* is an old dative meaning "to all"; you still hear it in the formal phrase *wszem wobec* (to one and all).
- *nad* + instrumental = above, over: *sen* (sleep) becomes *snem*.
- *czuwać* = to keep watch; *uśmiechnięta* = smiling (feminine).

## Dzisiaj w Betlejem: The Joyful Chorus Everyone Knows

*Dzisiaj w Betlejem* ("Today in Bethlehem") is anonymous, and its text appears in Father Jan Siedlecki's popular songbook of 1878. Of all the Polish Christmas carols, it is the one where even shy guests join in, because the chorus is a cheerful list of who is doing what at the stable. A carol with a similar tune and text is also known in Ukrainian and Belarusian tradition.

> Dzisiaj w Betlejem, dzisiaj w Betlejem wesoła nowina,
> Że Panna czysta, że Panna czysta porodziła Syna.
> Chrystus się rodzi, nas oswobodzi,
> Anieli grają, króle witają,
> Pasterze śpiewają, bydlęta klękają,
> Cuda, cuda ogłaszają.

**Meaning:** Today in Bethlehem there is happy news: the pure Virgin has given birth to a Son. Christ is born, He will set us free; angels play music, kings bow in welcome, shepherds sing, the animals kneel, and they all announce wonders, wonders.

Older printed songbooks have *Pan oswobodzi* ("the Lord will set free") where most people now sing *nas oswobodzi* ("will set us free"), a good example of how Polish Christmas carols lyrics shift slightly from book to book.

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *dzisiaj* = today, pronounced "JEE-shy". *Betlejem* does not change its ending: *w Betlejem*, *do Betlejem*.
- *nowina* = news, tidings; *porodziła* = gave birth (completed action, feminine).
- *anieli* is an older plural of *anioł* (angel); modern Polish says *anioły*.
- *bydlęta* = farm animals, cattle; *klękać* = to kneel.

## Przybieżeli do Betlejem: Shepherds at a Run

*Przybieżeli do Betlejem* ("They Came Running to Bethlehem") is one of the oldest Polish Christmas carols still sung. An early form of the text appears in Jan Żabczyc's collection *Symfonie anielskie* (Angelic Symphonies) of 1631, though without the familiar "Chwała" refrain. The text and melody were first printed together by Mioduszewski in 1843, and the version heard in churches today was fixed by Siedlecki's songbook in 1878.

> Przybieżeli do Betlejem pasterze,
> Grając skocznie Dzieciąteczku na lirze.
> Chwała na wysokości, chwała na wysokości,
> A pokój na ziemi.

**Meaning:** The shepherds came running to Bethlehem, playing a lively tune on the lyre for the tiny Child. Glory in the highest, glory in the highest, and peace on earth.

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *przybieżeli* is an old past-tense form; modern Polish would say *przybiegli* (they came running). Start it with "pshih-": *prz* is pronounced "psh".
- *skocznie* = in a bouncy, dance-like way, from *skoczny* (lively) and *skakać* (to jump).
- *Dzieciąteczko* is a double diminutive of *dziecko* (child), here in the dative: *Dzieciąteczku*.
- *chwała na wysokości* = glory in the highest, literally "glory on the height".

## Mizerna, cicha: A Poet's Gentle Kolęda

*Mizerna, cicha* ("Humble and Quiet") is younger than most Polish Christmas carols on this list. The words are by the Romantic poet **Teofil Lenartowicz** and were first published in 1849. The first tune was written by Father Jakub Wrzeciono, but the melody most people know today is the one by Jan Gall. It is slow and intimate, and a favourite of choirs and solo singers on albums of Polish Christmas carols.

> Mizerna, cicha,
> Stajenka licha,
> Pełna niebieskiej chwały.
> Oto leżący,
> Przed nami śpiący
> W promieniach Jezus mały.

**Meaning:** A poor, quiet, shabby little stable, yet full of heavenly glory. Here, lying before us and fast asleep, is little Jesus, bathed in rays of light.

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *mizerny* = meagre, poor-looking; *lichy* = shabby, flimsy. Both appear here in feminine form to match *stajenka*.
- *stajenka* = little stable, a diminutive of *stajnia*.
- *niebieski* = heavenly, and also the everyday word for "blue".
- *w promieniach*: locative plural of *promień* (ray).

## Pójdźmy wszyscy do stajenki: An Invitation in Song

*Pójdźmy wszyscy do stajenki* ("Let Us All Go to the Stable") has roots in the 17th century, while the text as sung today is from the 18th century and the march-like melody from the 19th. A 14-stanza version with a different tune was recorded in 18th-century Carmelite songbooks. Its author is unknown, and like many Polish Christmas carols it works as a simple invitation: come and see.

> Pójdźmy wszyscy do stajenki,
> Do Jezusa i Panienki,
> Powitajmy Maleńkiego
> I Maryję, Matkę Jego.

**Meaning:** Let's all go to the little stable, to Jesus and the Virgin Mary; let's welcome the little One, and Mary, His Mother.

**Vocabulary and pronunciation:**
- *pójdźmy* = let's go; *powitajmy* = let's welcome. The *-my* ending makes a "let's" command. *Pójdźmy* sounds like "POOJ-mih".
- *do* + genitive: *stajenka* becomes *do stajenki*, *Panienka* becomes *do Panienki*.
- *Panienka* (literally "young lady") is a traditional, affectionate name for the Virgin Mary in carols.

## When Poles Sing Kolędy: Wigilia, Pasterka and the Season Until 2 February

Polish Christmas carols have a season, and it is longer than many visitors expect. In families the singing usually starts on Christmas Eve, after the meatless supper, the sharing of the wafer and the gifts. If you want to know how that evening unfolds, our guide to [Wigilia, the Polish Christmas Eve](/blog/wigilia-polish-christmas-eve) walks through the whole evening, from the first star to the last dish.

For churchgoers, the high point for Polish Christmas carols is **Pasterka**, the solemn midnight Mass celebrated in the night from 24 to 25 December. The name comes from *pasterz* (shepherd): the Mass recalls the shepherds who hurried to Bethlehem in the night, which is exactly the story *Wśród nocnej ciszy* tells. It is one of the most important Christmas traditions in Poland, and for many churchgoers it is the first time that season they sing kolędy in full voice with hundreds of others.

Liturgically, Catholic carol singing runs from the midnight Mass to the Feast of the Baptism of the Lord in January. Polish custom stretches the season for Polish Christmas carols further, to **2 February**, the Feast of the Presentation of the Lord, known in Poland as the feast of *Matki Boskiej Gromnicznej* (Our Lady of the Candles), after the blessed candles called *gromnice*. This date closes the Christmas season: nativity scenes are taken down, trees come out of homes and the last kolędy are sung. So if you hear a carol in a Polish church at the end of January, nobody has forgotten to change the hymn list.

## Kolędnicy: Carolers With a Star, a Goat and a Turoń

Polish Christmas carols were never only church music. For centuries, from St Stephen's Day (26 December) to Epiphany (6 January), bands of *kolędnicy* (carolers) went from house to house with songs, rhymes and short comic plays, and collected food or coins as their "kolęda". The custom itself is far older than Christianity and is shared with other European peoples, as an article on the Polish government's [Powroty portal about caroling](https://powroty.gov.pl/piekny-zwyczaj-koledowania/) explains.

The most widespread group carried a **gwiazda**, a big star made from an old sieve hoop, with paper-covered points and often a candle inside. It was fixed on a pole and spun as the carolers sang. Other groups brought a puppet *szopka* (nativity scene), acted out the story of King Herod, or led a costumed animal.

That animal is where things get noisy. The **koza** (goat) had a carved head with a snapping jaw, and carolers recited rhymes such as *Gdzie koza chodzi, tam zboże rodzi* ("Where the goat walks, the grain grows"). The **turoń** was a horned, hairy beast with a clacking mouth that jumped around the room, teased the women of the house, "died" dramatically and was revived. Its visit was meant to bring a good harvest.

![A turoń mask from Stary Sącz, a horned caroler's beast with a snapping jaw made with real fur and hedgehog skin — Seweryn Udziela Ethnographic Museum in Kraków, CC0, via Wikimedia Commons](/blog/polish-christmas-carols-turon-mask.webp)

Today caroling survives in villages, at folklore festivals and in competitions for carol groups. If a group of kolędnicy knocks at your door, the polite thing is to listen, smile and give a small treat or a few coins.

## Scenario: Singing Kolędy at Wigilia

Here is how Polish Christmas carols come up in real life. Imagine you are at your partner's family table on Christmas Eve. The plates are cleared, and Grandma reaches for the songbook.

- **Ty:** Babciu, jaką kolędę teraz śpiewamy? *(Grandma, which carol are we singing now?)*
- **Babcia:** Zaczniemy od „Bóg się rodzi", a potem „Wśród nocnej ciszy". *(We'll start with "Bóg się rodzi", and then "Wśród nocnej ciszy".)*
- **Ty:** Nie znam wszystkich słów. *(I don't know all the words.)*
- **Babcia:** Nic nie szkodzi, śpiewaj z nami pierwszą zwrotkę. *(Never mind, sing the first verse with us.)*
- **Ty:** Do kiedy śpiewa się kolędy? *(Until when do people sing carols?)*
- **Babcia:** Do Matki Boskiej Gromnicznej, czyli do drugiego lutego. A o północy idziemy na Pasterkę. *(Until Candlemas, so until the second of February. And at midnight we're going to Pasterka.)*

Useful words for talking about Polish Christmas carols: *zwrotka* (verse, stanza), *refren* (chorus), *śpiewnik* (songbook), *kolędować* (to sing carols).

## Tips for Learning Polish Christmas Carols

Polish Christmas carols are some of the best pronunciation practice you will find, because the melody slows every syllable down. A few habits make them stick.

### Start With the Chorus

Most Polish Christmas carols lean on a refrain, and choruses repeat, so they are the easiest entry point. *Chwała na wysokości*, *Lulajże, Jezuniu, lulajże, lulaj* and *Chrystus się rodzi, nas oswobodzi* are short and memorable. Learn those first and you can join in on every round.

### Read the Text Aloud Before You Sing

Say each line slowly, then at speaking speed, then sing it. Watch for the sounds that trip English speakers: *sz* and *ś*, *cz* and *ć*, *rz* and *ż*, and nasal *ę* and *ą*. If those still feel shaky, the free PolishPal lesson on Polish sounds is a good warm-up:

[Polish Phonetics & Pronunciation](/lessons/phonetics)

### Collect the Vocabulary Families

Polish Christmas carols repeat the same word families: *pasterz / pastuszek / pastuszkowie*, *żłób / żłóbek*, *stajnia / stajenka*, *dziecko / Dzieciątko / Dzieciąteczko*. Grouping them shows you how Polish builds diminutives.

### Sing Along With Recordings

Search for a carol title plus *kolęda* and you will find church choirs, folk groups and pop singers. Hearing several versions teaches you which variants of Polish Christmas carols lyrics are common.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most famous Polish Christmas carol?

*Bóg się rodzi* is usually called the "queen" of Polish Christmas carols, thanks to its grand polonaise rhythm and Franciszek Karpiński's poetic text. *Wśród nocnej ciszy*, *Lulajże, Jezuniu* and *Dzisiaj w Betlejem* are equally well known, and *Cicha noc* is sung in almost every home.

### What does "kolędy" mean?

*Kolędy* is the plural of *kolęda*, a Polish Christmas carol. The word comes from the Latin *calendae* and once also meant New Year wishes and the gifts given to carolers. *Kolędować* means to go caroling.

### Are the lyrics of Polish Christmas carols public domain?

The traditional texts quoted here are old enough to be in the public domain, which is why digital libraries publish them freely. Be careful with modern English translations and new arrangements, though: those can be under copyright even when the original carol is not.

### How long do Poles sing kolędy?

Families sing Polish Christmas carols from the Christmas Eve supper and the midnight Mass (Pasterka) until 2 February, the Feast of the Presentation of the Lord. That date closes the Christmas season in Polish custom.

### Where can I find Polish Christmas carols lyrics with an English meaning?

This guide gives the first verse of nine Polish Christmas carols with our own English rendering. For the complete Polish texts, public-domain digital libraries such as Wolne Lektury are the most reliable source; songbooks sometimes differ by a word or two, so pick one version and stick with it while you learn.

### Did Chopin use one of the Polish Christmas carols in his music?

Yes. The slow middle section of Chopin's Scherzo No. 1 in B minor, Op. 20 quotes the melody of the carol *Lulajże, Jezuniu*.

Polish Christmas carols reward the effort you put into them. Learn one verse this year, sing it at a Wigilia table or along with a recording, and you will have a piece of Polish that comes back every December, together with a few hundred years of history and some of the most beautiful melodies the language has to offer.
