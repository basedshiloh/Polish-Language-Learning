On St Nicholas Day in Poland, children wake up on 6 December and reach straight for a shoe, a boot or the space under their pillow. Overnight, Święty Mikołaj has been. If they have behaved, there are sweets, a small toy or a chocolate figure. If not, there might be a bare twig.

In Polish, the day is called **Mikołajki** (mee-koh-WAY-kee), and it is one of the most loved dates in the Polish calendar, even though it is not a public holiday. Schools draw names for gift exchanges, offices run their own version, and shops fill with chocolate Santas weeks in advance. It is a small, cheerful warm-up for the bigger celebration on Christmas Eve.

This guide explains how St Nicholas Day in Poland works in practice: the shoes and pillows, the school *losowanie*, the rózga, the regional gift-bringers who compete with Mikołaj, the real bishop behind it all, and the Polish words you need to talk about it. And if you have searched for "Mikołajki" and found a lakeside resort instead, that is explained too.

## What Is Mikołajki? St Nicholas Day in Poland Explained

Mikołajki is the everyday Polish name for the feast of Saint Nicholas, celebrated on **6 December**. Polish Wikipedia describes it as the secularised name for St Nicholas's Day, and that is exactly how most families experience it: less a church feast than a children's morning of surprises.

The important part happens at night. According to [Poland's official Powroty (Returns) portal](https://powroty.gov.pl/mikolajki-piekny-zwyczaj-obdarowywania/), the key moment is the night from 5 to 6 December, when Mikołaj leaves small gifts under the pillow, in a sock or in a boot. In older practice, the visit came on the evening of 5 December.

A few quick facts about St Nicholas Day in Poland:

- **Date:** 6 December every year, whatever the day of the week.
- **Day off?** No. Schools and offices stay open, which is why so much of the fun happens there.
- **Who brings gifts:** Święty Mikołaj (Saint Nicholas), usually unseen.
- **What children get:** small things, mostly sweets.
- **Name day:** 6 December is also the *imieniny* of everyone called Mikołaj, so Polish Nicholases get double attention.

### Not to Be Confused with the Town of Mikołajki

Type "Mikołajki" into a search engine and you may get weather forecasts, hotels and an aqua park. That is because Mikołajki is also a town in Masuria, in Poland's Warmian-Masurian Voivodeship, set where Lake Tałty meets Lake Mikołajskie. It is a popular sailing base, sometimes called the sailing capital of Poland.

The two share a patron. The town's name comes from its church, which is dedicated to St Nicholas. So whether you are reading about the town or the holiday, Mikołaj is behind it. This article is about St Nicholas Day in Poland, the holiday on 6 December; if you are planning a summer on the Masurian lakes, you want the other Mikołajki.

## Gifts in Shoes, Boots and Under the Pillow

The heart of St Nicholas Day in Poland is the morning discovery. Children put out a shoe or a boot before bed, or simply go to sleep knowing that Mikołaj knows where to find them. Polish sources describe three classic hiding places:

- **w bucie** (in a shoe or boot),
- **w skarpecie** (in a sock, usually a big one),
- **pod poduszką** (under the pillow).

Some parents go further. The Powroty portal mentions families who draw white boot prints on the floor or carpet, as if Mikołaj had walked in from the snow. Younger children often write a letter to St Nicholas first, *list do Świętego Mikołaja*, listing what they would like.

The gifts themselves are modest, because Mikołajki is about small surprises. Polish Wikipedia notes that they are mainly sweets, and in the past well-behaved children received fruit, nuts, cakes or sweets. Today you might find chocolate, a small book, a toy car, warm socks or a figure of Mikołaj wrapped in red foil.

## Small Gifts Now, Big Presents at Wigilia

Visitors who first experience St Nicholas Day in Poland are sometimes confused: if Mikołaj comes on 6 December, who brings the presents at Christmas? In many Polish homes, the answer is Mikołaj again.

The two visits work differently. Gifts on St Nicholas Day in Poland are small and personal, often hidden in a shoe. The main presents arrive under the Christmas tree on 24 December, after the Christmas Eve supper, and they are usually larger and shared out among the whole family. Mikołajki is the teaser; Wigilia is the main event.

That second evening has its own rich set of rules, from the first star to the empty place at the table. Our guide to [Wigilia, the Polish Christmas Eve](/blog/wigilia-polish-christmas-eve) explains how the presents fit into the night. In some regions, as you will see below, the Christmas Eve gifts come from someone else entirely.

## The Rózga: A Twig for Naughty Children

Every Polish child knows the threat: *Jak będziesz niegrzeczny, dostaniesz rózgę!* (If you're naughty, you'll get the rózga!). A **rózga** (ROOZ-gah) is a thin, flexible twig or a bundle of them, and it is the punishment gift for children who have misbehaved.

In practice, few children ever find a twig instead of chocolate. More often, a small rózga sits beside the sweets as a joke and a warning, or parents mention it all through November to improve behaviour. It is the Polish version of the coal and switches that appear in other European St Nicholas traditions.

The idea is old. Polish Wikipedia records that historically, naughty children could be struck with the bishop's crosier rather than rewarded. In Greater Poland, ethnographic accounts cited by the history portal Dzieje.pl describe the regional gift-bringer Gwiazdor handing out rods and rotten potatoes, *zgniłe pyry*, to children who had not behaved. Next to that, a symbolic twig is a mild fate.

## Mikołajki at School and Work: The Losowanie

Because Mikołajki is a normal working day, St Nicholas Day in Poland largely moves into classrooms and offices. The centre of it is the **losowanie** (loh-soh-VAH-nyeh), the draw.

A week or two before 6 December, a class or team puts everyone's names into a hat. Each person draws one name in secret and buys that person a small gift. Polish Wikipedia notes that participants are called *mikołajki*, and the person you are buying for is your *mikołajek*. On the day, gifts are handed out together, and part of the fun is guessing who drew you.

The rules are familiar from Secret Santa:

- a price limit agreed in advance, so nobody feels awkward,
- one gift per person, wrapped and labelled with the recipient's name,
- secrecy until the gifts are opened.

On 6 December, kindergartens and primary schools often go further, with a visit from someone dressed as Mikołaj handing out sweets. Many workplaces hold their own *losowanie*, and for adults learning Polish, an office Mikołajki is one of the easiest ways to join in. You only need a few phrases, all covered in the scenario below.

## Who Brings the Gifts? Mikołaj, Gwiazdor, Dzieciątko and Aniołek

On St Nicholas Day itself, Mikołaj rules the whole country, from the mountains to the sea. Christmas Eve is a different story. Depending on the region, and often on the family, the Wigilia presents may come from a different figure. Sources do not agree on every border, so treat this as a rough map rather than a rule.

| Gift-bringer | Pronunciation | Who it is | Where you are most likely to meet him |
|---|---|---|---|
| Święty Mikołaj | SHFYEN-tih mee-KOH-wahy | Saint Nicholas | Everywhere on 6 December; in most other regions on 24 December too |
| Gwiazdor | GVYAHZ-dor | "the Star Man" | Greater Poland (Wielkopolska), also reported in Warmia |
| Dzieciątko | jeh-CHOHNT-koh | the Christ Child | Silesia |
| Gwiazdka | GVYAHST-kah | "the Little Star" | Lower Silesia and parts of southern Poland |
| Aniołek | ah-NYOH-wek | "the Little Angel" | Parts of Lesser Poland (Małopolska) and smaller towns and villages |

**Gwiazdor** is the most distinctive. Ethnologist Andrzej Brencz, quoted by Dzieje.pl, calls him a typically Greater Poland figure, a stern judge rather than a jolly Santa, whose tradition was alive in the 19th century. He grew out of carol-singing groups, one of whom carried a star on a pole. Traditional descriptions give him a sheepskin coat, a fur hat and a face hidden by a mask or soot.

**Dzieciątko** (the Christ Child) and **Aniołek** (the Little Angel) are gentler and usually invisible. Children rarely see them; the presents simply appear under the tree. Brencz describes Aniołek as a transformed version of the Gwiazdor tradition. For 6 December the regions agree, but on 24 December the answer to "Who brought this?" can change from one family to the next.

## The Real St Nicholas of Myra

Behind the red-coated gift-bringer of St Nicholas Day in Poland stands a real bishop. Nicholas lived in the 3rd and 4th centuries and served as bishop of Myra in Lycia, in today's south-western Turkey. Tradition places his birth in Patara around 270 and his death on 6 December, which is why his feast falls on that date. The [Saint Nicholas article on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Nicholas) notes that the earliest accounts of his life were written centuries after his death, so much of his story is legend.

The best-known legend explains the gifts. A poor man could not afford dowries for his three daughters, and Nicholas secretly threw money through the window at night so that they could marry. Secret, night-time giving became his trademark, and over time he became the patron saint of children, sailors and many others.

In 1087, sailors from Bari carried most of his relics from Myra to Italy, where they are still kept in the Basilica di San Nicola. His cult spread across both Eastern and Western Christianity, and Poland was no exception: the cheerful St Nicholas Day in Poland today grew out of that old church feast.

### A Bishop, Not a Santa

Look at a traditional Polish Mikołaj and you will see the difference from the North American Santa Claus. Polish Wikipedia lists his attributes as a grey beard, a **pastorał** (crosier), a **mitra** (mitre) and a long red bishop's robe.

On St Nicholas Day, some parishes and schools still welcome this bishop version, while the Santa-style Mikołaj with a red hat and a sack dominates shopping centres. Polish uses the same name for both, *Święty Mikołaj*, which can confuse learners. Context, and the presence or absence of a mitre, tells you which one you are looking at.

## Where the Word "Mikołajki" Comes From

The name is built on **Mikołaj**, the Polish form of Nicholas, with the plural ending *-ki*. The same pattern gives Polish other festive names: **Andrzejki** for St Andrew's Eve on 29 November, a night of wax-pouring and fortune-telling covered in our guide to [Andrzejki, the Polish fortune-telling tradition](/blog/andrzejki-polish-tradition), and **Walentynki** for Valentine's Day.

Because it is plural, the word behaves like one: *W tym roku Mikołajki są w niedzielę* (This year St Nicholas Day falls on a Sunday, as it does in 2026), and *na Mikołajki* (for St Nicholas Day). The genitive plural is *Mikołajek*, which is why you may see *Wesołych Mikołajek!* (Happy St Nicholas Day!) on cards and social media.

You may read that the origin of the word is disputed. We could not find a reliable source that says so: the reference sources we checked simply treat Mikołajki as the name of St Nicholas's Day, derived from Mikołaj. The more interesting twist is that the same word is used for the gift exchange itself, and for the people taking part in it.

## Scenario: Mikołajki at Home and at the Office

Here is how St Nicholas Day in Poland sounds in two everyday settings.

**At home, 6 December, 7 a.m.** Zosia, aged six, runs to the hallway where she left her boot.

> **Mama:** Zosiu, zobacz, co jest w twoim bucie!
> *Zosia, look what's in your boot!*
>
> **Zosia:** Czekoladki! Mikołaj tu był!
> *Chocolates! Mikołaj was here!*
>
> **Mama:** Widzisz? Byłaś grzeczna.
> *You see? You were good.*
>
> **Zosia:** A Kuba dostał rózgę?
> *And did Kuba get the twig?*
>
> **Mama:** Nie, Kuba też był grzeczny. Prawie.
> *No, Kuba was good too. Almost.*

**At the office, a week earlier.** Ben, a British developer in Wrocław, is taking part in his first *losowanie*.

> **Ania:** Kogo wylosowałeś na Mikołajki?
> *Whose name did you draw for Mikołajki?*
>
> **Ben:** Nie mogę powiedzieć! To tajemnica.
> *I can't say! It's a secret.*
>
> **Ania:** Pamiętasz, że limit to pięćdziesiąt złotych?
> *You remember the limit is fifty złoty?*
>
> **Ben:** Pamiętam. W Anglii Mikołaj przychodzi dopiero w Boże Narodzenie.
> *I remember. In England, Father Christmas only comes at Christmas.*
>
> **Ania:** U nas przychodzi szóstego grudnia. Zostawia słodycze w bucie albo pod poduszką.
> *Here he comes on 6 December. He leaves sweets in a shoe or under the pillow.*
>
> **Ben:** A jeśli ktoś był niegrzeczny?
> *And if someone was naughty?*
>
> **Ania:** Dostaje rózgę!
> *They get the twig!*

## Frequently Asked Questions

### When is St Nicholas Day in Poland?

St Nicholas Day, Mikołajki, is on 6 December every year. Children usually find their gifts on the morning of the 6th, after Mikołaj's visit during the night from 5 to 6 December.

### Is Mikołajki a public holiday in Poland?

No. St Nicholas Day in Poland is a normal working and school day, which is why classes and offices hold their own gift exchanges.

### What do Polish children get on Mikołajki?

On St Nicholas Day, Polish children get small gifts, mostly sweets, sometimes a little toy, a book or socks. They are left in a shoe or boot, in a sock or under the pillow. The bigger presents come on Christmas Eve.

### What happens on St Nicholas Day in Poland?

The main traditions of St Nicholas Day in Poland are gifts left overnight in a shoe or under the pillow, the rózga for naughty children, and the school or office *losowanie* gift exchange.

### What is a rózga?

A thin twig or small bundle of twigs given, in theory, to naughty children instead of sweets. Today it is mostly a joke or a gentle warning.

### Who brings presents at Christmas in Poland?

It depends on the region and the family. Święty Mikołaj is the most common, but Greater Poland has Gwiazdor, Silesia has Dzieciątko (the Christ Child), and parts of southern Poland have Aniołek (the Little Angel) or Gwiazdka (the Little Star).

### Is Mikołajki the same as the town in Masuria?

No, but they are related. Mikołajki the town takes its name from its St Nicholas church. Mikołajki the holiday is St Nicholas Day in Poland, on 6 December.

### How do you say "Happy St Nicholas Day" in Polish?

You can say *Wesołych Mikołajek!* It is informal and cheerful, and you will see it on cards, in classrooms and on social media around 6 December.

For a learner, St Nicholas Day in Poland is a gift in itself: a short, cheerful day built on a handful of words you can use at once. Learn *rózga*, *losowanie* and *Co dostałeś?*, put a chocolate in a colleague's shoe, and you will have taken part in one of Poland's friendliest December customs.
