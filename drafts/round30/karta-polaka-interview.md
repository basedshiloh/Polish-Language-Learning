The Karta Polaka interview is the part of the application that worries people most, and the part law firms say least about. Eligibility guides explain documents and ancestry in detail. Then they add one line: "you will also need to show basic Polish." In practice, that one line is the Karta Polaka interview: a conversation, held in Polish, with a Polish consul, and it covers your language and your knowledge of Polish traditions and customs.

This guide gives a short, accurate overview of who can get a Karta Polaka, then concentrates on what we know best: how to prepare for the Karta Polaka interview as a language learner. You will find what "basic knowledge of Polish" means in practice, the topics worth preparing, phrases with English translations, a sample conversation and a study plan.

> **Rules change. Confirm with the official body.** Everything below is based on the [Act on the Pole's Card](https://isap.sejm.gov.pl/isap.nsf/DocDetails.xsp?id=WDU20260000076) (*Ustawa o Karcie Polaka*) as consolidated in January 2026 and on gov.pl consulate pages. Procedures, booking systems and timelines differ between consulates. Check your own consulate's page before you apply. This article is practical guidance, not legal advice.

## What the Karta Polaka Is, in Brief

Before the Karta Polaka interview itself, a quick overview. The Karta Polaka (the Pole's Card) is a document confirming that its holder belongs to the Polish Nation. It is **not** Polish citizenship. The Act says plainly that it is not a document for crossing the border or settling in Poland. It cannot be granted to someone who already holds Polish citizenship or a permanent residence permit in Poland.

Until 2019 the card was aimed at Poles in the former Soviet states. An amendment in force since July 2019 removed that limit, and gov.pl consulate pages from Portugal to the United States now explain how to apply.

### Who can apply

Under Article 2 of the Act, an applicant must declare that they belong to the Polish Nation and meet all of these conditions:

1. show a connection with Polishness through **at least basic knowledge of Polish**, which they regard as their native language, and knowledge and practice of Polish traditions and customs;
2. sign a written declaration of belonging to the Polish Nation in the presence of the consul;
3. show that they are of Polish nationality, or that at least one parent or grandparent, or two great-grandparents, were of Polish nationality, **or** present a certificate from an authorised Polish or Polonia organisation confirming at least three years of active work for the Polish language, culture or the Polish minority;
4. declare that neither they nor their ancestors were repatriated from Poland under the 1944–1957 agreements with the Soviet republics.

Proving ancestry is a documents question: birth and marriage records, baptism certificates, old Polish IDs, military records and similar. That is where specialist advisers earn their fees, and this article will not try to replace them.

### Cost, decision and timing

The application is **free**. Article 12 of the Act exempts every step, including consular fees. The decision is made by the **consul** responsible for your place of residence. For applicants from certain countries named in a government regulation, a voivode in Poland can handle the case instead.

Consulate pages state that a decision should normally take no more than two months. If the consul refuses, you can appeal to the Council for Poles Abroad (*Rada do Spraw Polaków poza Granicami Kraju*). The card is valid for ten years and can be renewed. A card granted to someone aged 65 or over is valid indefinitely.

### What it gives you

The card lets you work in Poland without a work permit and run a business on the same terms as Polish citizens. It also gives you access to study and research, emergency healthcare, a 37% discount on single train tickets, free entry to selected state museums, and exemption from some consular fees. Holders who move to Poland can apply for permanent residence as card holders. After a year of that permanent residence, they can apply to be recognised as citizens, but that step needs a formal B1 language document, which our guide to the [Polish B1 exam](/blog/polish-b1-exam) explains.

## How the Karta Polaka Interview Works

The first application must be filed **in person**, because two things happen in front of the consul: you sign the declaration of belonging to the Polish Nation, and you show your Polish in a conversation. The [gov.pl consulate pages](https://www.gov.pl/web/usa/karta-polaka-informacje-ogolne) say the Karta Polaka interview is held **in Polish** and covers, among other things, Polish traditions and customs.

Booking a Karta Polaka interview differs by consulate. Some use the e-Konsulat online system. Others, such as the Consulate General in New York, ask you to book Karta Polaka appointments by email or phone. Read your consulate's page carefully and bring every original document plus copies.

What we do not know matters just as much. No official question list, script or score exists for the Karta Polaka interview. The consul assesses your Polish in the conversation and decides. Treat any website selling "the official Karta Polaka interview questions" with suspicion. No official list has ever been published.

### Who may not need the language conversation

Article 13 of the Act lists documents that prove Polish on their own:

- a state certificate in Polish as a foreign language, issued under Article 11a of the Polish Language Act;
- a school or university certificate from Poland;
- a school certificate from abroad where Polish was the language of instruction.

If you have none of these, the consul assesses your Polish during the Karta Polaka interview itself. The consulate pages add one warning: **a certificate of completing a Polish course is not enough**. That covers certificates from language schools and online courses too.

## The Karta Polaka Language Requirement in Practice

The Karta Polaka language requirement is short on paper. The Act asks for "at least basic knowledge" (*przynajmniej podstawowa znajomość*) of Polish. It does not translate that into a CEFR level such as A1 or A2, and no official guidance we found does either. So there is no number to aim at, only a skill.

In practice, a successful Karta Polaka interview looks like a normal, slow, polite conversation. You should be able to:

- greet the consul, introduce yourself and answer questions about your age, work, family and where you live;
- explain in simple sentences who your Polish ancestors were and where they came from;
- say why you are applying and what being Polish means to you;
- describe how your family celebrates Christmas Eve, Easter and other holidays;
- answer simple questions about Polish symbols, holidays and history;
- ask for repetition or slower speech when you lose the thread, in Polish.

That is roughly where a learner sits after a solid A1 and part of A2. Our practical advice is to aim for a comfortable A2 before you book your Karta Polaka interview. That target is ours, not an official threshold, but it gives you a margin for nerves, a fast speaker or an unexpected question.

The Act's wording also matters. It describes Polish as a language the applicant "regards as their native language", in other words as part of identity rather than a tourist skill. Personal, specific answers ("my grandmother taught me to make *pierogi* for Wigilia") are likely to serve you better than textbook sentences.

## Topics to Prepare for the Karta Polaka Interview

Because traditions and customs are named on the consulate pages, prepare those carefully for the Karta Polaka interview. The list below is our suggestion, built from that official description and the topics any conversation about Polish identity tends to reach.

### You and why you are applying

At the start of a Karta Polaka interview, expect to talk about yourself first: name, age, job, where you live, who you live with. Then the big one: *Dlaczego chce Pan / chce Pani mieć Kartę Polaka?* (Why do you want the Karta Polaka?). Prepare an honest answer of four or five sentences that mentions your family, your connection to Poland and what you plan to do with the card.

### Your family history

This is the heart of the Karta Polaka interview, and it should be the easiest part, because it is your story. Learn to say who your Polish ancestors were, where they were born, when and why they left, and how Polish survived in your family. Practise place names with the right prepositions and cases: *w Krakowie*, *z Tarnowa*, *pod Rzeszowem*.

![Old family photographs spread across open albums, the kind of family history worth preparing to talk about in Polish — Photo by Thiago José Amaral on Pexels](/blog/karta-polaka-interview-family-history.webp)

### Christmas: Wigilia

Christmas Eve is the most important family celebration in Polish tradition, and a natural Karta Polaka interview topic. Know the key words: *Wigilia*, *opłatek* (the wafer shared before the meal), *pierwsza gwiazdka* (the first star that signals the start), the empty place set for an unexpected guest, *barszcz z uszkami*, *pierogi*, carols (*kolędy*) and midnight Mass (*Pasterka*). Our guide to [Wigilia, the Polish Christmas Eve](/blog/wigilia-polish-christmas-eve) covers each custom in detail.

![Christmas wafers (opłatek) with a fir branch and a gold star, the wafer Polish families share on Christmas Eve — Image by _Alicja_ from Pixabay](/blog/karta-polaka-interview-oplatek.webp)

### Easter: Wielkanoc

Easter is the other great family holiday. In the Karta Polaka interview, be ready to describe the *święconka*, the basket of food blessed in church on Holy Saturday, and *śmigus-dyngus*, the water fight on Easter Monday. The article on [Wielkanoc and Polish Easter traditions](/blog/wielkanoc-polish-easter-traditions) gives you the vocabulary and the story behind each custom.

### National holidays and symbols

Two state holidays are worth knowing well: 3 May, Constitution Day, marking the 1791 Constitution, and 11 November, National Independence Day, marking the return of independence in 1918. Our article on [Polish Independence Day](/blog/polish-independence-day) explains why 11 November matters so much.

Learn the three national symbols too: the white eagle with a crown on a red background (*godło*), the white-and-red flag (*flaga*), and the national anthem, *Mazurek Dąbrowskiego*, which opens with *Jeszcze Polska nie zginęła* ("Poland has not yet perished"). You do not need to sing it at the consulate, but you should recognise it and know what the first line means.

### Key moments in Polish history

The Karta Polaka interview is not a history exam, but a consul may ask a simple question to see whether Poland's story means something to you. Know the outline: the baptism of Poland in 966, the 1791 Constitution, the partitions that wiped Poland off the map for 123 years, independence in 1918, the Second World War, Solidarity in 1980 and the changes of 1989. For the partition years, our story of [how Polish survived 123 years without a country](/blog/how-polish-survived-123-years-without-a-country) is a good read, because it is also a story about families like yours keeping the language alive.

### Everyday Polish culture

Rounding out your preparation, be able to name a few famous Poles you admire and say why (for example Chopin, Maria Skłodowska-Curie, Adam Mickiewicz or Pope John Paul II). Mention a Polish dish you cook, a custom your family keeps, or a place in Poland you have visited or want to visit. Personal details make the conversation easier for you and give the consul a clearer picture of your connection to Poland.

## Useful Phrases for the Karta Polaka Interview

If you address the consul directly, say *Panie Konsulu* to a man or *Pani Konsul* to a woman, and use the polite *Pan* / *Pani* form throughout. Where a phrase changes for a man or a woman, both forms are shown.

| Polish | English |
|---|---|
| Dzień dobry. Nazywam się Anna Kowalska. | Good morning. My name is Anna Kowalska. |
| Przepraszam, czy może Pan / Pani powtórzyć? | Sorry, could you repeat that? |
| Proszę mówić trochę wolniej. | Please speak a little more slowly. |
| Nie zrozumiałem / Nie zrozumiałam pytania. | I didn't understand the question. (man / woman) |
| Uczę się polskiego od dwóch lat. | I have been learning Polish for two years. |
| W domu mówiliśmy po polsku. | At home we spoke Polish. |
| Moja babcia urodziła się w Krakowie. | My grandmother was born in Kraków. |
| Mój pradziadek pochodził ze wsi pod Rzeszowem. | My great-grandfather came from a village near Rzeszów. |
| Chcę mieć Kartę Polaka, bo czuję się Polakiem / Polką. | I want the Karta Polaka because I feel Polish. (man / woman) |
| W Wigilię dzielimy się opłatkiem. | On Christmas Eve we share the wafer. |
| W Wielką Sobotę idziemy ze święconką do kościoła. | On Holy Saturday we take the Easter basket to church. |
| Jedenastego listopada obchodzimy Narodowe Święto Niepodległości. | On 11 November we celebrate National Independence Day. |
| Godłem Polski jest biały orzeł w koronie na czerwonym tle. | Poland's emblem is a crowned white eagle on a red background. |
| Nie jestem pewien / pewna, ale myślę, że… | I'm not sure, but I think that… (man / woman) |
| Nie wiem, ale chętnie się dowiem. | I don't know, but I'll gladly find out. |

The last two phrases are your Karta Polaka interview safety net. If you do not know an answer, saying so calmly in Polish shows more real language than a memorised speech.

## Scenario: A Sample Conversation With the Consul

This is an illustration of the kind of exchange you can practise, not an official script.

> **Konsul:** Dzień dobry, proszę usiąść. Dlaczego chce Pani mieć Kartę Polaka?
> *(Good morning, please sit down. Why do you want the Karta Polaka?)*
>
> **Ty:** Dzień dobry. Moi dziadkowie byli Polakami. W domu zawsze obchodziliśmy polskie święta i chcę zachować ten związek z Polską.
> *(Good morning. My grandparents were Polish. At home we always celebrated Polish holidays, and I want to keep this connection with Poland.)*
>
> **Konsul:** Skąd pochodzili Pani dziadkowie?
> *(Where did your grandparents come from?)*
>
> **Ty:** Babcia pochodziła z Tarnowa, a dziadek ze wsi pod Rzeszowem.
> *(Grandma came from Tarnów, and Grandpa from a village near Rzeszów.)*
>
> **Konsul:** Jak Pani rodzina spędza Wigilię?
> *(How does your family spend Christmas Eve?)*
>
> **Ty:** Czekamy na pierwszą gwiazdkę, potem dzielimy się opłatkiem i składamy sobie życzenia. Na stole jest barszcz z uszkami i pierogi.
> *(We wait for the first star, then we share the wafer and exchange wishes. On the table there is beetroot soup with little dumplings, and pierogi.)*
>
> **Konsul:** A kiedy obchodzimy Święto Niepodległości?
> *(And when do we celebrate Independence Day?)*
>
> **Ty:** Jedenastego listopada. W tysiąc dziewięćset osiemnastym roku Polska odzyskała niepodległość.
> *(On 11 November. In 1918 Poland regained its independence.)*

Notice that every answer is short, concrete and personal. That is the style to practise for your own Karta Polaka interview.

## A Prep Plan for the Karta Polaka Interview

How long you need to prepare for the Karta Polaka interview depends on where you start. A heritage speaker who grew up hearing Polish may need a few weeks of polishing. A complete beginner should plan on several months of steady study before booking. A rough plan:

### Phase 1: Foundations

If you are starting from zero, build the basics first: pronunciation, introductions, the verb *być*, numbers, dates and family vocabulary. Learn to talk about the present and, as soon as you can, the past tense, because your family history is all in the past.

### Phase 2: Your own story

Write your answers to the core Karta Polaka interview questions in Polish: who you are, why you are applying, who your ancestors were, how your family keeps Polish traditions. Have a teacher or a Polish-speaking relative correct them. Then practise saying them aloud until they sound natural rather than recited.

### Phase 3: Traditions, symbols and history

Read about Wigilia, Wielkanoc, 3 May and 11 November, and learn ten to fifteen key words for each. Learn the symbols and the first line of the anthem, since national symbols are an easy Karta Polaka interview question. Build a one-page timeline of Polish history with one simple Polish sentence per date.

### Phase 4: Mock interviews

Ask a Polish speaker to play the consul and to ask questions you have not prepared. Practise recovering: asking for repetition, saying you are not sure, rephrasing. Do at least three mock Karta Polaka interview sessions before your appointment, ideally with different people.

On the day of your Karta Polaka interview, arrive early with your documents in order, greet the consul in Polish, and speak slowly. Nerves are normal, so give yourself time to think before you answer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is the Karta Polaka interview in Polish or English?

In Polish. The gov.pl consulate pages state that the conversation with the consul is conducted in Polish and covers, among other things, Polish traditions and customs.

### What level of Polish do I need for the Karta Polaka?

The Act requires "at least basic knowledge" and does not name a CEFR level. A comfortable A2 is our recommended target for a relaxed Karta Polaka interview, not an official requirement.

### Can I skip the Karta Polaka interview with a certificate?

A state certificate in Polish as a foreign language, or a school or university certificate from Poland or from a Polish-language school abroad, proves your Polish under the Act. You still have to file in person and sign the declaration before the consul. A certificate from an ordinary language course does not count.

### Is the Karta Polaka free?

Yes. Article 12 of the Act makes the application and every related step free of charge, including consular fees. Your own costs, such as obtaining old records or translations, are separate.

### What happens if I fail the Karta Polaka interview?

If the consul finds you do not meet the conditions, they issue a refusal decision. You can appeal to the Council for Poles Abroad. Check the refusal letter for the appeal deadline and procedure, and ask the consulate about applying again once you have studied more.

### Does the Karta Polaka give me Polish citizenship?

No. The Act says granting the card does not mean acquiring Polish citizenship. It can be a step on the way, through permanent residence and later recognition as a citizen, which has its own B1 language requirement.

A Karta Polaka interview is not an exam you pass by cramming. It is a conversation about your family and your connection to Poland, in a language that has hopefully been part of that story for generations. Prepare your story, learn the traditions behind it, and practise telling it in Polish.
