Polish prepositions are short words with a surprisingly big job: each one decides which case the noun after it must take. In English, "to", "in" and "with" leave the next word alone. In Polish, *do* (to) pulls the noun into the genitive, *z* takes the genitive or the instrumental depending on what it means, and *w* (in) and *na* (on) switch case depending on whether something is moving or staying put.

That sounds like a lot to memorise, but there is a clear system behind it. Most Polish prepositions only ever take one case, a small group takes two, and most of that two-case group follows one reliable rule about motion and location. Once you see the pattern, prepositions stop being random and start doing half of your case work for you.

This guide gives you a complete Polish prepositions list with the case each one governs, minimal pairs that show motion versus location, the tricky *z*, *w* and *na* choices, time expressions, the extra *-e* in *we* and *ze*, and a practice set with answers.

## How Polish Prepositions Work: One Preposition, One Case

Polish nouns change their endings depending on their role in a sentence. If you need a refresher on what cases are and what the core endings look like, the grammar reference below covers the four cases taught in the PolishPal course.

[The Cases — Overview](/grammar/cases-overview)

Here is the key idea for Polish prepositions: **the preposition chooses the case, not you.** After *do* you always use the genitive. After *przy* you always use the locative. After *dzięki* you always use the dative. You do not need to think about the noun's role in the sentence, because the preposition has already made the decision.

Look at one noun, *szkoła* (school), after five different prepositions:

| Phrase | Preposition | Case | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| do szkoły | do | genitive (dopełniacz) | to school |
| w szkole | w | locative (miejscownik) | at / in school |
| obok szkoły | obok | genitive (dopełniacz) | next to the school |
| przed szkołą | przed | instrumental (narzędnik) | in front of the school |
| za szkołą | za | instrumental (narzędnik) | behind the school |

The noun is the same, the meaning of "school" is the same, but the ending changes because each of these Polish prepositions demands its own case. This is why learners who memorise prepositions together with their case make faster progress than learners who memorise them as single English words.

### Which Cases Do Polish Prepositions Use?

Polish prepositions use five of the seven cases: the genitive, dative, accusative, instrumental and locative. The nominative (the dictionary form) and the vocative (used for addressing someone) almost never follow a preposition. The locative is special, because it never appears without one. It exists only after *w*, *na*, *o*, *po* and *przy*.

### Pronouns After Prepositions

Third-person pronouns gain an *n-* after a preposition. On its own, "him" is *jego* or *go*, but after a preposition it becomes *niego*: *do niego* (to him), *dla niego* (for him). The same happens with *nim* (*z nim*, with him), *niej* (*o niej*, about her) and *nich* (*u nich*, at their place). It is a small detail, but native speakers notice immediately when it is missing.

### Polish Prepositions With the Genitive

The genitive group is the biggest. Notice how many everyday Polish prepositions take it: *do, od, z* (from), *bez, dla, u, obok, koło, podczas*. If you are unsure and the preposition is about origin, destination, absence or "for someone", the genitive is the best bet, so it pays to know the genitive endings well before you drill these.

### Polish Prepositions With the Dative

The dative group is the smallest. *Dzięki, przeciw / przeciwko* and *ku* are the main Polish prepositions that take it. *Ku* sounds formal or literary today and survives in set phrases such as *ku mojemu zaskoczeniu* (to my surprise). *Przeciw* and *przeciwko* mean the same thing and are both correct.

### Locative-Only: Przy

Among the Polish prepositions on this list, *przy* is the only common one that takes the locative and nothing else. It means "by", "at" or "next to": *przy stole* (at the table), *przy oknie* (by the window). You can also hear it in friendly phrases such as *pogadamy przy kawie* (we'll chat over coffee).

## Polish Prepositions Cases: Motion vs Location

The most important rule about Polish prepositions cases concerns the seven prepositions that switch case for motion and location: *w, na, za, pod, nad, przed* and *między*. (*O* and *po* also take two cases, but there the choice depends on meaning, as the list above shows.) The choice depends on one question: **is something moving towards a place, or is it already there?**

- Movement towards a place answers *Dokąd?* (Where to?) and takes the **accusative**.
- Being in a place answers *Gdzie?* (Where?) and takes the **locative** (after *w*, *na*) or the **instrumental** (after *za*, *pod*, *nad*, *przed*, *między*).

### W and Na: Accusative vs Locative

With *w* and *na*, motion uses the accusative and position uses the locative.

| Motion (accusative) | Position (locative) |
|---|---|
| Kładę książkę na stół. (I'm putting the book onto the table.) | Książka leży na stole. (The book is lying on the table.) |
| Idę na pocztę. (I'm going to the post office.) | Jestem na poczcie. (I'm at the post office.) |
| Jedziemy w góry. (We're going to the mountains.) | Jesteśmy w górach. (We're in the mountains.) |
| Jadę na Węgry. (I'm going to Hungary.) | Mieszkam na Węgrzech. (I live in Hungary.) |

One important warning: with *w*, the accusative-of-motion pattern is limited. For most buildings, cities and countries, Polish uses *do* + genitive for movement instead: *Idę do szkoły* (I'm going to school), *Jadę do Krakowa* (I'm going to Kraków). Motion with *w* + accusative is mainly heard with mountains (*w góry, w Tatry*) and set phrases such as *wyruszyć w drogę* (to set off on a journey).

### Za, Pod, Nad, Przed, Między: Accusative vs Instrumental

These five work the same way, but position takes the instrumental. This is where knowing the instrumental endings pays off, because every "position" example below uses them.

[Instrumental Case](/grammar/instrumental)

| Preposition | Motion (accusative) | Position (instrumental) |
|---|---|---|
| pod (under) | Kot wchodzi pod stół. (The cat goes under the table.) | Kot śpi pod stołem. (The cat sleeps under the table.) |
| za (behind) | Piłka wpadła za kanapę. (The ball fell behind the sofa.) | Piłka leży za kanapą. (The ball is lying behind the sofa.) |
| nad (by water, above) | Jedziemy nad morze. (We're going to the seaside.) | Mieszkamy nad morzem. (We live by the sea.) |
| przed (in front of) | Samochód podjechał przed dom. (The car pulled up in front of the house.) | Samochód stoi przed domem. (The car is parked in front of the house.) |
| między (between) | Wkładam list między książki. (I'm slipping the letter between the books.) | List leży między książkami. (The letter is lying between the books.) |

In daily life, *pod*, *za* and *nad* are the Polish prepositions you will hear in both cases constantly. Motion with *przed* and *między* is correct but less frequent, so if you are a beginner, it is fine to learn their instrumental "position" use first.

*Nad* has a special everyday meaning with water. *Jedziemy nad morze* means "we're going to the seaside", and *nad jezioro* means "to the lake". Once you arrive, it is *nad morzem* and *nad jeziorem*.

## Z + Genitive vs Z + Instrumental

Of all the Polish prepositions, the little word *z* causes the most confusion, because it is really two prepositions that happen to look the same.

| Z + genitive (from, off, out of) | Z + instrumental (with) |
|---|---|
| Jestem z Polski. (I'm from Poland.) | kawa z mlekiem (coffee with milk) |
| Wracam z pracy. (I'm coming back from work.) | Idę do kina z bratem. (I'm going to the cinema with my brother.) |
| Zdejmij kubek ze stołu. (Take the mug off the table.) | Rozmawiam z mamą. (I'm talking with Mum.) |
| sok z jabłek (juice made from apples) | pierogi z mięsem (pierogi with meat) |

The easiest test is the question you could ask. *Skąd?* (From where?) or "made of what?" points to the genitive. *Z kim?* (With whom?) or *Z czym?* (With what?) points to the instrumental.

![Milk being poured into a cup of coffee, the classic example of z + instrumental: kawa z mlekiem — Photo by Feyza Daştan on Pexels](/blog/polish-prepositions-coffee-milk.webp)

### Od vs Z for "From"

English uses "from" for both people and places. Polish splits them. Use *z* for places and *od* for people: *Wracam z Krakowa* (I'm coming back from Kraków), but *Wracam od babci* (I'm coming back from Grandma's). Both take the genitive.

### Where To, Where, Where From

The most useful table in this guide may be this one. It shows the three questions of movement for the three most common types of destination.

| Type of place | Dokąd? (Where to?) | Gdzie? (Where?) | Skąd? (Where from?) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A "w" place (shop, school, city) | do szkoły | w szkole | ze szkoły |
| An "na" place (post office, station) | na pocztę | na poczcie | z poczty |
| A person | do babci | u babci | od babci |

Learn this grid with one noun from each row and you can build most everyday sentences about going places with Polish prepositions. Notice that the "from" column always uses the genitive, whichever row you are in.

## W vs Na: Polish Prepositions for Places

Choosing between *w* and *na* for "in / at" is partly logical and partly a matter of memorising the right Polish prepositions for each place. The general pattern: *w* is for enclosed spaces, buildings, cities and most countries, while *na* is for open spaces, events, certain institutions and some regions and countries.

| W + locative | Na + locative |
|---|---|
| w sklepie (in the shop) | na poczcie (at the post office) |
| w szkole (at school) | na dworcu (at the station) |
| w kinie (at the cinema) | na lotnisku (at the airport) |
| w domu (at home) | na uniwersytecie (at university) |
| w pracy (at work) | na koncercie (at a concert) |
| w Warszawie (in Warsaw) | na lekcji (in a lesson) |
| w Polsce (in Poland) | na plaży (on the beach) |
| we Francji (in France) | na wsi (in the countryside) |
| w Niemczech (in Germany) | na Węgrzech (in Hungary) |
| w Czechach (in Czechia) | na Słowacji (in Slovakia) |
| w górach (in the mountains) | na Śląsku (in Silesia) |

Countries with *na* are a small, historical group: *na Węgrzech, na Słowacji, na Litwie* (in Lithuania) and *na Łotwie* (in Latvia) are the ones you will hear most. For almost every other country, *w* is correct.

![The Poczta Polska post office building in Bielsko-Biała with its green dome, a reminder that you are na poczcie, not w poczcie — Image by ProPolski from Pixabay](/blog/polish-prepositions-post-office.webp)

### Do vs Na for Destinations

The *w*/*na* split carries over to movement. If a place takes *w* for location, it usually takes *do* + genitive for destination. If it takes *na* for location, it takes *na* + accusative for destination.

| Location | Destination |
|---|---|
| w sklepie | do sklepu |
| w Polsce | do Polski |
| w domu | do domu |
| na poczcie | na pocztę |
| na dworcu | na dworzec |
| na lotnisku | na lotnisko |
| na koncercie | na koncert |
| na wsi | na wieś |

So if you already know that you are *na lotnisku* at the airport, you can work out that you are going *na lotnisko*, not *do lotniska*. This single trick fixes a huge number of beginner errors with Polish prepositions.

## Polish Prepositions of Time

Time expressions use many of the same Polish prepositions, often with a different meaning. The PolishPal reference on clock times shows how the hour changes after *o*, so it pairs well with this section.

[Telling the Time](/grammar/telling-time)

| Preposition + case | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| o + locative | at (clock time) | o piątej (at five), o północy (at midnight) |
| w + accusative | on (days of the week) | w poniedziałek, w sobotę, we wtorek |
| w + locative | in (months, years) | w styczniu (in January), w 2026 roku (in 2026) |
| za + accusative | in (from now) | za tydzień (in a week), za pięć minut (in five minutes) |
| przed + instrumental | before | przed obiadem (before lunch), przed pracą (before work) |
| po + locative | after | po obiedzie (after lunch), po pracy (after work) |
| od … do + genitive | from … to / until | od poniedziałku do piątku (from Monday to Friday) |
| przez + accusative | for (duration) | przez godzinę (for an hour) |
| na + accusative | for (planned length) | Jadę do Krakowa na tydzień. (I'm going to Kraków for a week.) |
| podczas + genitive | during | podczas lekcji (during the lesson) |

### Za, Przez, Na: Three Ways to Say "A Week"

These three Polish prepositions are easy to mix up, so compare them side by side:

- *Za tydzień* jadę do Polski. (I'm going to Poland **in** a week.)
- Byłem w Polsce *przez tydzień*. (I was in Poland **for** a week.)
- Jadę do Polski *na tydzień*. (I'm going to Poland **for** a week, as a planned stay.)

To say "a week ago", Polish puts the word *temu* after the noun: *tydzień temu*. You will also see the more formal *przed tygodniem* with the same meaning.

## The Inserted -e: We, Ze, Ze Mną, We Wtorek

Some Polish prepositions grow an extra *-e* when the next word would be hard to pronounce. You cannot pronounce *w Wrocławiu* cleanly, so Polish writes and says *we Wrocławiu*.

### W Becomes We

*W* usually becomes *we* before a word that starts with *w* or *f* followed by another consonant:

- *we Wrocławiu* (in Wrocław)
- *we Francji* (in France)
- *we Włoszech* (in Italy)
- *we wtorek* (on Tuesday)
- *we wrześniu* (in September)
- *we mnie* (in me)

Compare *w środę* (on Wednesday) and *w czwartek* (on Thursday), which keep the plain *w*. Only *wtorek* among the days of the week needs *we*.

### Z Becomes Ze

*Z* becomes *ze* before a word starting with *s, z, ś, ź, sz* or *ż* followed by another consonant, and in a few fixed combinations such as *ze mną* and *ze wszystkimi*:

- *ze Szczecina* (from Szczecin)
- *ze szkoły* (from school)
- *ze stołu* (off the table)
- *ze mną* (with me)
- *ze wszystkimi* (with everyone)

### The Mnie / Mną Family

The pronoun *mnie / mną* triggers the extra *-e* on several other Polish prepositions as well: *ode mnie* (from me), *beze mnie* (without me), *nade mną* (above me), *pode mną* (below me), *przede mną* (in front of me) and *przeze mnie* (because of me). The preposition *o* is an exception and stays short: *o mnie* (about me).

## Scenario: Asking for Directions

Here is how these Polish prepositions sound in a short, realistic conversation in a Polish town.

> **Turysta:** Przepraszam, gdzie jest poczta?
> *(Excuse me, where is the post office?)*
>
> **Pani:** Poczta jest obok kościoła, za bankiem.
> *(The post office is next to the church, behind the bank.)*
>
> **Turysta:** A jak tam dojść?
> *(And how do I get there?)*
>
> **Pani:** Proszę iść prosto przez park, a potem w lewo.
> *(Go straight through the park, and then turn left.)*
>
> **Turysta:** Dziękuję! Najpierw idę na pocztę, a potem do apteki.
> *(Thank you! First I'm going to the post office, and then to the pharmacy.)*
>
> **Pani:** Apteka jest przy rynku. Miłego dnia!
> *(The pharmacy is by the market square. Have a nice day!)*

Count the cases: *obok kościoła* (genitive), *za bankiem* (instrumental), *przez park* (accusative), *na pocztę* (accusative of motion), *do apteki* (genitive), *przy rynku* (locative). Six prepositions and four different cases, all in one ordinary exchange.

## Practice: Choose the Right Case

Test your Polish prepositions: put the noun in brackets into the correct form. The answers are below, so cover them first.

1. Idę do ___ . (sklep)
2. Mieszkam w ___ . (Kraków)
3. Poproszę kawę z ___ . (mleko)
4. Jestem z ___ . (Polska)
5. Kot śpi pod ___ . (stół)
6. Kot wchodzi pod ___ . (stół)
7. W sobotę jedziemy nad ___ . (morze)
8. Spotkajmy się o ___ . (piąta)
9. Muszę iść na ___ . (poczta)
10. Piję herbatę bez ___ . (cukier)
11. Wszystko się udało dzięki ___ . (ty)
12. Idziesz ___ mną do kina? (z / ze)

### Answers

1. **sklepu**: *do* + genitive.
2. **Krakowie**: *w* + locative, position.
3. **mlekiem**: *z* meaning "with" + instrumental.
4. **Polski**: *z* meaning "from" + genitive.
5. **stołem**: *pod* + instrumental, position.
6. **stół**: *pod* + accusative, motion.
7. **morze**: *nad* + accusative, motion towards the seaside.
8. **piątej**: *o* + locative for clock time.
9. **pocztę**: *na* + accusative, motion.
10. **cukru**: *bez* + genitive.
11. **tobie**: *dzięki* + dative.
12. **ze**: *z* becomes *ze* before *mną*.

If you got question 6 or 7 wrong, reread the motion-versus-location section. That pair of rules is where most learners lose points, and it is also where you will feel the most progress once it clicks. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Which Polish prepositions should I learn first?

Start with *w, na, do, z, od, u, o, po, przy* and *bez*. These ten cover location, destination, origin, people's homes, "about", "after" and "without", which is most of what a beginner needs. Add the motion-versus-location pairs for *pod, za* and *nad* next.

### Do Polish prepositions ever take the nominative?

In normal use, no. Two fixed patterns are worth knowing, though. In the question *Co to za…?* (What kind of … is this?), the noun stays in the nominative: *Co to za książka?* And in clock times such as *za dziesięć dziewiąta* (ten to nine), the hour stays in the nominative too.

### How do I know whether a place takes w or na?

There is a general pattern (*w* for enclosed spaces and cities, *na* for open spaces, events and some institutions), but many places simply have to be learned. The best habit is to learn every new place word with its preposition already attached: *na poczcie, w sklepie, na dworcu*.

### Why do Polish people say "ze" and "we" instead of "z" and "w"?

It is purely about pronunciation, and it affects only a handful of Polish prepositions. The extra *-e* appears when the next word starts with a consonant cluster that would be hard to say after a bare consonant, as in *we Wrocławiu* or *ze szkoły*. The meaning and the case do not change.

### Is "przeciw" or "przeciwko" correct?

Both are correct, and both take the dative: *przeciw wojnie* or *przeciwko wojnie* (against war). If you want to see how a dictionary lays out a single preposition's cases and meanings, the [Wiktionary entry for the Polish preposition w](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/w#Polish) is a good example, with separate senses for the accusative and the locative.

Polish prepositions are where the case system becomes practical. Every time you say where you are, where you are going or who you are with, a preposition is quietly choosing your ending. Learn each one with its case, drill the motion-versus-location pairs, and the rest of Polish grammar starts to feel far less mysterious.
