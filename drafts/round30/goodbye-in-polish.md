The standard way to say goodbye in Polish is *do widzenia* (doh vee-DZEH-nyah), the polite farewell you can use with anyone. Among friends, though, you'll hear *cześć*, *na razie*, *pa* and a dozen other options, and at night everything shifts to *dobranoc*. Choosing between them follows the same logic as Polish greetings: are you on first-name terms or not?

Polish farewells also do more work than English ones. A single goodbye in Polish can promise to meet tomorrow, wish someone a good evening, send them off on a journey or close a phone call. There are set phrases for each, and a few small traps, like the wrong ending in *miłego wieczoru* or a *żegnam* that sounds colder than you meant.

This guide gives you every common farewell with an English-friendly phonetic spelling, sorted by situation and formality. You'll also get the etiquette around leaving a party or a shop, the bedtime wishes Polish parents say to their kids, and a short dialogue showing how a long Polish goodbye actually unfolds.

## How to Say Goodbye in Polish: The Quick Answer

Here's every common goodbye in Polish, sorted from most formal to most casual. If you only learn two, make them *do widzenia* and *cześć*.

| Polish | Sounds like | Register | Literal meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do widzenia | doh vee-DZEH-nyah | Formal / neutral | Until seeing |
| Do zobaczenia | doh zoh-bah-CHEH-nyah | Neutral | Until we see each other |
| Cześć | cheshch | Informal | Hi / bye |
| Na razie | nah RAH-zheh | Informal | For now |
| Trzymaj się | TSHY-my sheh | Informal | Hold on (take care) |
| Pa / pa pa | pah / pah pah | Casual, affectionate | Bye / bye-bye |
| Nara | NAH-rah | Slang | Short for *na razie* |
| Dobranoc | doh-BRAH-nots | Any (at night) | Good night |

If you've already read our guide to [hello in Polish](/blog/hello-in-polish), the formal and informal split will feel familiar. The same people who get *dzień dobry* from you get *do widzenia*; the same people who get *cześć* on arrival get *cześć*, *na razie* or *pa* on departure.

## Do Widzenia: The Polite Goodbye in Polish

*Do widzenia* is the neutral, all-purpose goodbye in Polish. It works with shop assistants, taxi drivers, doctors, teachers, officials, older neighbours and anyone you'd address as *Pan* or *Pani*. Polish linguists describe it as the unmarked farewell, the one that carries no special emotional colouring.

### How to pronounce do widzenia

Break it into four syllables: **doh vee-DZEH-nyah**. The *w* is an English "v." The *dz* is a single sound, like the "ds" in "beds." The *ni* before a vowel makes a soft "ny," as in "canyon." Stress the second-to-last syllable, *DZE*, as with almost every Polish word.

Literally it means "until seeing," and it's used even when you'll never see the person again. Saying *do widzenia* to a cashier in a city you're leaving tomorrow is completely normal; it's a formula, not a promise.

### Where do widzenia is expected

Poles say goodbye in places where many English speakers just walk out. Leaving a small shop, a bakery, a pharmacy, a doctor's surgery or a lift, a quick *do widzenia* is the polite reflex. It's also how you end a formal phone call and how pupils say goodbye to teachers.

To make it more personal, add the formal "you": *Do widzenia panu* (to a man), *Do widzenia pani* (to a woman) or *Do widzenia Państwu* (to a group). You'll hear these most from older people and in more traditional settings.

## Do Zobaczenia, Do Jutra and Other "Until" Ways to Say Goodbye in Polish

A surprising number of ways to say goodbye in Polish are built on the word *do*, meaning "until." They're friendly, specific and very common.

| Polish | Sounds like | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Do zobaczenia | doh zoh-bah-CHEH-nyah | See you |
| Do zobaczenia wkrótce | doh zoh-bah-CHEH-nyah FKROOT-tseh | See you soon |
| Do jutra | doh YOO-trah | See you tomorrow |
| Do poniedziałku | doh poh-nyeh-JAHW-koo | See you on Monday |
| Do piątku | doh PYONT-koo | See you on Friday |
| Do wieczora | doh vyeh-CHOH-rah | See you this evening |
| Do usłyszenia | doh oo-swy-SHEH-nyah | Talk to you soon (on the phone) |
| Do napisania | doh nah-pee-SAH-nyah | Write soon (in chats, colloquial) |

*Do zobaczenia* is a touch warmer than *do widzenia* because it suggests you really will meet again. It works with colleagues, classmates and acquaintances, and you can use it with people you address formally too: *Do zobaczenia w czwartek, pani Ewo* (See you on Thursday, Ms Ewa).

### The grammar behind do jutra

*Do* is always followed by the genitive case, which is why *jutro* (tomorrow) becomes *jutra* and *poniedziałek* (Monday) becomes *poniedziałku*. You don't need to master the genitive to use these phrases, but if you want to build your own (*do soboty*, see you on Saturday; *do niedzieli*, see you on Sunday), the genitive reference shows the endings.

## Informal Ways to Say Goodbye in Polish

With friends, family and anyone on first-name terms, the formal goodbye in Polish sounds stiff. Here's what Poles actually say instead.

### Cześć works both ways

The same *cześć* (**cheshch**) that means "hi" also means "bye." Context makes it obvious. It's the most common informal goodbye in Polish, suitable for friends, colleagues your age and classmates.

### Na razie and nara

*Na razie* (**nah RAH-zheh**) literally means "for now," so it's close to "see you later" or "laters." The *zi* in *razie* is the soft Polish "zh" sound, so the word comes out as **RAH-zheh**, with a lightly tapped r at the start. It's always written as two words; *narazie* is a very common misspelling.

Younger Poles often clip it to *nara* (**NAH-rah**), which is pure slang. Use it with friends your own age and nowhere else.

### Pa, pa pa and buziaki

*Pa* (**pah**) is a short, warm "bye." Doubled as *pa pa*, it becomes softer and more affectionate, the kind of goodbye in Polish you'd use with children, your partner, your mum on the phone or close friends. Adults use it constantly at the end of calls: *No to pa!* ("Well, bye then!").

In messages to close friends and family, people often sign off with *buziaki* (**boo-ZHAH-kee**, "kisses") or *buziaczki*, the even cuter diminutive. These are strictly for people you'd happily hug.

### Trzymaj się

*Trzymaj się* (**TSHY-my sheh**) literally means "hold yourself," and it's the Polish "take care." It's warm and informal, often used when someone is going through a tough time or when you won't see them for a while. To a group, say *Trzymajcie się* (**tshy-MY-cheh sheh**).

Is there a special way to say goodbye in Polish to a girl? Not really: all of these phrases are the same whether you're talking to a man or a woman. With a girlfriend, the warmth comes from what you add, like *Pa, kochanie* (bye, darling) or *Do jutra, skarbie* (see you tomorrow, sweetheart).

## Dobranoc: How to Say Good Night in Polish

*Dobranoc* (**doh-BRAH-nots**) is the one word for good night in Polish, written as a single word. You say it when leaving in the late evening, when someone is going to bed, and at the end of a late phone call or chat. Like the English "good night," it's a farewell only, never a greeting.

As a goodbye in Polish, it also suits every register. You can say *dobranoc* to your child, to a friend, and to the neighbour you pass on the stairs at 11 p.m., though with a stranger leaving a shop late in the evening, *do widzenia* is still the safer choice.

### Bedtime wishes beyond dobranoc

Polish has a lovely set of bedtime phrases, used with children, partners and close family.

| Polish | Sounds like | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Dobranoc | doh-BRAH-nots | Good night |
| Dobrej nocy | DOB-rey NOH-tsy | Have a good night |
| Śpij dobrze | shpeey DOB-zheh | Sleep well |
| Śpij słodko | shpeey SWOT-koh | Sleep sweetly |
| Słodkich snów | SWOT-keekh snoof | Sweet dreams |
| Kolorowych snów | koh-loh-ROH-vykh snoof | Colourful dreams |
| Miłych snów | MEE-wykh snoof | Pleasant dreams |

*Kolorowych snów*, "colourful dreams," is the one English speakers don't have. It's especially common with children. Notice that the dream wishes all end in *snów*, the genitive plural of *sen* (dream), because they're short for "I wish you…".

![A parent's hand stroking a child's hair as she lies in bed at night, the moment for good night in Polish: dobranoc, kolorowych snów — Photo by Artem Podrez on Pexels](/blog/goodbye-in-polish-dobranoc-bedtime.webp)

Generations of Polish children also associate *dobranoc* with *Dobranocka*, the short evening cartoon slot that has aired on Polish public television since its early days. As [Polish Wikipedia's entry on Dobranocka](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dobranocka) notes, classics like *Bolek i Lolek* and *Miś Uszatek* were made for it, and it was traditionally shown around 7 p.m., just before bedtime.

## Miłego Dnia: A Goodbye in Polish That Wishes You Well

Many a goodbye in Polish is really a wish. They're built on an unspoken *życzę* ("I wish"), which is why the noun after them changes to the genitive.

| Polish | Sounds like | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Miłego dnia | mee-WEH-goh dnyah | Have a nice day |
| Miłego wieczoru | mee-WEH-goh vyeh-CHOH-roo | Have a nice evening |
| Miłego weekendu | mee-WEH-goh wee-KEN-doo | Have a nice weekend |
| Wszystkiego dobrego | fshist-KYEH-goh doh-BREH-goh | All the best |
| Powodzenia | poh-voh-DZEH-nyah | Good luck |
| Szerokiej drogi | sheh-ROH-kyey DROH-gee | Safe travels (literally "wide road") |
| Szczęśliwej podróży | shchen-SHLEE-vey pod-ROO-zhy | Have a good trip |

*Miłego dnia* is relatively new in its everyday role. A Polish etiquette guide drawing on linguist Andrzej Markowski lists *Miłego dnia życzę* as a recent formula inspired by English "have a nice day." Today you'll hear it from shop staff, call-centre agents and friends alike.

Watch the ending in *miłego wieczoru*. Polish has two genitive forms of *wieczór*, and it's easy to reach for *wieczora* because of the set phrase *od rana do wieczora* (from morning till evening). *Do wieczora* (see you this evening) is correct, but the National Centre for Culture's language column is clear that [*miłego wieczoru* is the only correct form](https://nck.pl/projekty-kulturalne/projekty/ojczysty-dodaj-do-ulubionych/ciekawostki-jezykowe/od-rana-do-wieczora-milego-wieczoru) of this wish.

The standard reply to any of these is *Nawzajem* (**nah-VZAH-yem**, "likewise" or "you too"), or *Dziękuję, nawzajem* (thank you, you too).

## Goodbye in Polish on the Phone and in Writing

### Ending a phone call

On the phone, the standard goodbye in Polish is *do usłyszenia* ("until we hear each other") rather than *do zobaczenia*. It's polite enough for business calls and friendly enough for family. Casual calls tend to wind down with a string of little words: *No dobra, to na razie, pa!* ("Okay then, bye for now, bye!"). Polish phone goodbyes often take several rounds of *pa* before anyone actually hangs up.

### Signing off emails and messages

A written goodbye in Polish has its own set of sign-offs, roughly from formal to friendly:

| Polish | Sounds like | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Z poważaniem | z poh-vah-ZHAH-nyem | Formal letters, official emails (like "Yours faithfully") |
| Z wyrazami szacunku | z vy-RAH-zah-mee shah-TSOON-koo | Very formal, "with expressions of respect" |
| Pozdrawiam | poz-DRAH-vyahm | Everyday emails, "regards" |
| Serdecznie pozdrawiam | ser-DECH-nyeh poz-DRAH-vyahm | Warmer, "warm regards" |
| Do usłyszenia / do zobaczenia | as above | Friendly emails when you'll speak or meet soon |
| Pa / buziaki / nara | as above | Texts to friends and family only |

*Pozdrawiam* is the workhorse. It's polite, neutral and appropriate for most work emails once the first formal exchange is over.

## Żegnam and Żegnaj: A Goodbye in Polish With an Edge

The verb *żegnać* means "to bid farewell," and its forms sound weightier than any other goodbye in Polish covered above. *Żegnaj* (**ZHEG-nai**) is the dramatic, final farewell of films, poems and songs, closer to "farewell" or "goodbye forever." You won't say it to someone you'll see next week.

*Żegnam* (**ZHEG-nahm**, "I bid you farewell") is trickier. It's formal and a little old-fashioned, and Polish usage guides list it among the stylistically marked farewells rather than the neutral ones. In practice it can sound distant or curt; a sharp *Żegnam pana!* is how someone ends an argument and walks out. Learners should stick with *do widzenia*.

![Józef Brandt's painting "Do widzenia" showing a rider waving his cap to a woman in the doorway of a thatched cottage, a 19th-century goodbye in Polish art — Józef Brandt, public domain, via Wikimedia Commons](/blog/goodbye-in-polish-brandt-do-widzenia.webp)

Older farewells survive too. *Z Bogiem* (**z BOH-gyem**, "go with God") is still heard from grandparents, and the painter Józef Brandt (1841–1915), famous for his battle scenes and horses, even titled one of his paintings simply *Do widzenia*.

## Goodbye Etiquette: How Poles Actually Leave

Saying the words is the easy part. Here's how a goodbye in Polish works in practice.

**Leaving a party takes time.** It's polite to find the hosts, thank them and say goodbye individually rather than slipping out. Typical phrases are *Dziękujemy za gościnę* (**jen-koo-YEH-my zah gosh-CHEE-neh**, "thank you for your hospitality") and *Było bardzo miło* ("It was really lovely"). Hosts often reply with *Wpadajcie częściej!* ("Drop by more often!").

**The doorway still matters.** The same superstition that stops Poles greeting across a threshold applies to goodbyes: say your farewells and hug inside, or step outside first.

**Kisses and hugs mirror the greeting.** If you greeted someone with a cheek kiss or a hug, expect the same on parting. A handshake on arrival usually means a handshake on departure.

**Shops and small rooms.** As with *dzień dobry* on the way in, a quick *do widzenia* on the way out of a shop, a lift or a waiting room is expected.

## Common Mistakes When Saying Goodbye in Polish

These are the slips that most often make a goodbye in Polish sound slightly off.

1. **Using *dobranoc* as a greeting.** Arriving at 10 p.m., you say *dobry wieczór*. *Dobranoc* is only for leaving or bedtime.

2. **Saying *miłego wieczora*.** The wish is always *miłego wieczoru*.

3. **Saying *pa* or *nara* to your boss.** Both are for friends and family. Formal relationships get *do widzenia*.

4. **Using *do widzenia* with close friends.** Not wrong, but it can sound cold or jokey. Use *cześć*, *na razie* or *pa*.

5. **Reaching for *żegnam* to sound polite.** It often sounds final or frosty. *Do widzenia* is the polite option.

6. **Writing *narazie*.** It's two words: *na razie*.

7. **Forgetting the genitive after *do*.** It's *do jutra*, not *do jutro*, and *do poniedziałku*, not *do poniedziałek*.

## Scenario: Saying Goodbye in Polish After a Family Dinner

You've spent the evening at your Polish friend Marta's family home. It's late, and you're getting ready to leave. Notice how many different goodbyes appear in a single exit.

> **You:** *No, na mnie już czas. Dziękuję za pyszną kolację!*
> (Well, it's time for me to go. Thank you for the delicious dinner!)
>
> **Marta's mum:** *Ależ proszę! Było nam bardzo miło. Wpadaj częściej!*
> (Oh, you're welcome! It was lovely to have you. Drop by more often!)
>
> **You:** *Na pewno. Do widzenia pani, do widzenia panu!*
> (I definitely will. Goodbye, ma'am; goodbye, sir!)
>
> **Marta's dad:** *Do widzenia, szerokiej drogi!*
> (Goodbye, safe travels!)
>
> **Marta (at the door):** *Napisz, jak dojedziesz. Pa, trzymaj się!*
> (Text me when you get home. Bye, take care!)
>
> **You:** *Jasne. Dobranoc, do jutra!*
> (Sure. Good night, see you tomorrow!)

You used formal *do widzenia* with Marta's parents and informal *pa* and *do jutra* with Marta, all in the same doorway. That switching is completely normal and is exactly what makes your Polish sound natural. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do you say goodbye in Polish formally?

Say *do widzenia* (doh vee-DZEH-nyah). For extra politeness, add *panu* (to a man), *pani* (to a woman) or *Państwu* (to a group). On the phone, use *do usłyszenia*, and in a formal email, sign off with *Z poważaniem*.

### How do you say goodbye in Polish informally?

With friends, say *cześć*, *na razie*, *pa* or *trzymaj się*. Young people also use the slang *nara*. If you'll see them soon, *do jutra* (see you tomorrow) or *do zobaczenia* (see you) are friendly and natural.

### How do you say goodbye in Polish phonetically?

*Do widzenia* sounds like **doh vee-DZEH-nyah**, *do zobaczenia* like **doh zoh-bah-CHEH-nyah**, *na razie* like **nah RAH-zheh**, *cześć* like **cheshch**, and *pa* like **pah**. Stress the second-to-last syllable in each longer word.

### How do you say good night in Polish?

*Dobranoc* (doh-BRAH-nots). For bedtime, add *śpij dobrze* (sleep well), *słodkich snów* (sweet dreams) or the very Polish *kolorowych snów* (colourful dreams).

### What does "na razie" mean?

Literally "for now." As a farewell it means "see you later" or "bye for now." It's informal, and it's always written as two words.

### Is pa a rude goodbye in Polish?

No, it's affectionate, but it's informal. *Pa* and *pa pa* are perfect with friends, family and partners, and slightly too familiar for your boss, a shop assistant or someone you've just met. With them, use *do widzenia*.

### Does cześć mean hello or goodbye?

Both. *Cześć* is the informal word for "hi" and "bye," and context makes it clear which one you mean. Its formal counterparts are *dzień dobry* for hello and *do widzenia* for goodbye.

### How do you say goodbye in Polish to a girlfriend or boyfriend?

The words don't change with gender, so use *pa*, *pa pa* or *do jutra*, and add a term of endearment: *Pa, kochanie* (bye, darling) or *Dobranoc, skarbie* (good night, sweetheart).

The best way to make these stick is to attach each one to a real exit. Say *do widzenia* to the next shopkeeper, *na razie* to a friend and *dobranoc* before bed, out loud if you can. Within a week, the right goodbye in Polish will come to you before you've even reached the door.
