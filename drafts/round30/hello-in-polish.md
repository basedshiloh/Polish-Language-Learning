The two words you need for hello in Polish are *cześć* (cheshch) for friends and *dzień dobry* (jen DOH-bry) for everyone else. That split between informal and formal is the whole game. Pick the right one and you sound polite and natural; pick the wrong one and a perfectly friendly greeting can land as either stiff or a little cheeky.

This guide to hello in Polish goes further than a phrasebook list. You'll get English-friendly phonetic spellings for every greeting, the rules for choosing between them, and the situations where Polish greetings work differently from English ones: answering the phone, walking into a doctor's waiting room, greeting a whole group, or deciding whether to shake a hand or lean in for a cheek kiss.

You'll also find the slangier options younger Poles use with each other (*hej*, *siema*, *elo*), the old-fashioned and religious greetings you'll still hear in villages and mining towns, and the long-running argument over whether *witam* is polite or presumptuous. By the end, saying hello in Polish should feel like a decision you can make in half a second.

## The Two Main Ways to Say Hello in Polish

If you remember nothing else, remember this table. Almost every greeting decision comes down to whether you'd call the other person by their first name.

| Polish | Sounds like | Register | Use it with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dzień dobry | jen DOH-bry | Formal / neutral | Strangers, shop staff, older people, teachers, neighbours, anyone you'd call Pan or Pani |
| Cześć | cheshch | Informal | Friends, family, classmates, colleagues your age, anyone on first-name terms |
| Dobry wieczór | DOH-bry VYEH-choor | Formal / neutral | The evening version of *dzień dobry* |
| Hej | hey | Casual | Friends, younger people, messages |

The default hello in Polish for a visitor is *dzień dobry*. Nobody will ever be offended by it, and Poles will switch you to *cześć* themselves when they're ready. Going the other way, using *cześć* with someone who expected *dzień dobry*, is the one greeting mistake that genuinely stands out.

## Dzień Dobry: The Polite Way to Say Hello in Polish

*Dzień dobry* literally means "good day," and it is the formal hello in Polish you'll hear most often in public. It works from early morning until roughly the evening, it suits every age group, and it carries just the right amount of respect for people you don't know.

### How to pronounce dzień dobry

The tricky part is *dzień*. The letters *dzi* make a soft "j" sound, a bit like the start of "jeans" with the tongue pressed further forward. The *ń* is a soft "ny," as in "canyon." Put together, *dzień* comes out close to "jen," with a slight "y" glide at the end. *Dobry* is two syllables, DOH-bry, with a lightly tapped Polish "r."

So the whole phrase sounds like **jen DOH-bry**. Don't pronounce it "dee-zen" or "dzee-en": the written *dz* and *i* merge into one sound, made with the middle of the tongue raised towards the roof of the mouth.

### Where dzień dobry is expected

In Poland, a polite hello in Polish is expected in places where many English speakers would stay silent. Walking into a small shop, a pharmacy, a bakery or a doctor's waiting room, it's normal to say it to the room in general. Neighbours greet each other with it on the stairs and in the lift. Children say it to every adult they know who isn't family, from teachers to their friends' parents.

The person entering a room is the one who greets those already inside, even if the people inside are more senior. That's a standard rule of Polish etiquette, and it explains why a quiet *dzień dobry* as you push open a door is such a reflex for Poles.

You can make it warmer or more personal by adding a name or a title: *Dzień dobry, pani Aniu* (good morning, Ms Ania) or *Dzień dobry, panie doktorze* (good morning, doctor). The title switches to its "calling" form, the vocative, which is why *pan* becomes *panie* and *Ania* becomes *Aniu*.

### Good morning in Polish

Here's something that surprises learners: there is no separate everyday phrase for good morning in Polish. *Dzień dobry* covers it. You say it to the barista at 7 a.m., to your colleagues at 9, and to the postman at 2 p.m. If you search for a dedicated morning greeting, you'll find literal translations like *dobry ranek*, but they aren't what Poles actually say.

At home and between close people, good morning in Polish is still *dzień dobry*, just said differently: softer, sleepier, often with a pet name. *Dzień dobry, kochanie* (good morning, darling) is a classic. In casual speech you'll also hear the clipped *Dobry!*, which is friendly but brisk, a bit like "Mornin'!"

![White stencilled letters on a Lublin pavement reading "Dzień dobry rybko" (good morning, little fish), a playful way to say hello in Polish — Photo: Grzegorz W. Tężycki, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons](/blog/hello-in-polish-dzien-dobry-rybko.webp)

That pavement message in Lublin is a lovely example. *Rybko* is the calling form of *rybka*, "little fish," one of many affectionate diminutives Poles use for partners and children. Our guide to [Polish diminutives](/blog/polish-diminutives) explains how these little endings change a word's warmth.

### Dobry wieczór for the evening

Once evening arrives, *dzień dobry* gives way to *dobry wieczór* (**DOH-bry VYEH-choor**), "good evening." There is no official switchover hour. In practice people move to it once the working day is over and it's getting dark, which in a Polish winter can be mid-afternoon and in June can be quite late.

One thing to avoid: *dobranoc* means "good night" and is only a goodbye or a bedtime wish. It is never a way to say hello in Polish, even late at night.

## Cześć: The Everyday Informal Hello in Polish

*Cześć* is the friendly, informal hello in Polish, and it's also used for goodbye. Historically it's the same word as the noun *cześć*, meaning "honour" or "respect," which is why it once had a slightly elevated feel. Today it's simply "hi" (and "bye") between people on first-name terms.

### How to pronounce cześć

On paper it looks like a wall of consonants. Broken down, it's easy:

- **cz** = "ch" as in "church"
- **e** = "e" as in "bed"
- **ść** = a soft "sh" followed by a soft "ch"

Together: **cheshch**. Say "fresh cheese" quickly and listen to the "sh-ch" in the middle; that's the ending. In relaxed speech, many Poles shorten it to something closer to "chesh," and you won't sound wrong if you do the same. What you should avoid is "chess" or "check," which drop the soft sounds that make the word recognisable.

### Who you can say cześć to

Use *cześć* with friends, family, classmates, flatmates and colleagues of your own generation, plus anyone who has told you to use their first name. In some workplaces, particularly young teams, first names and *cześć* can be the norm from day one.

Avoid it with strangers, shop staff, your partner's parents (until invited), older neighbours, teachers and bosses who haven't set a first-name tone. A child who says *cześć* to a teacher is being cheeky, and an adult learner who does it reads the same way, even if people forgive it as a foreigner's slip.

Is there a special way to say hello in Polish to a girl? No. *Cześć* and *hej* work the same for everyone, because greetings in Polish don't change for gender. What does change is the form of a name when you add it: Polish uses a special calling form, so Kasia becomes *Cześć, Kasiu!* and Tomek becomes *Cześć, Tomku!*

## Hej, Siema and Other Casual Ways to Say Hello in Polish

Below *cześć* sits a layer of even more casual greetings, heard among friends and all over group chats and social media. Use them once you're comfortable with people, not as a first hello.

| Polish | Sounds like | Feel |
|---|---|---|
| Hej | hey | Casual, friendly, used by all ages, very common in messages |
| Hejka | HAY-kah | Cute, playful version of *hej* |
| Siema | SHEH-mah | Slangy "hey" or "yo," especially among younger people |
| Siemka | SHEM-kah | Softer, friendlier *siema* |
| Siemanko | sheh-MAHN-koh | Jokey, extra-casual *siema* |
| Elo | EH-loh | Youth slang, roughly "yo" |
| Czołem | CHOH-wem | Old-fashioned or jokey, a bit like "greetings!" |

*Siema* has a neat backstory: it's a clipped version of *jak się masz?* ("how are you?"), worn down over time to *się ma(sz)* and then *siema*. Our guide to [Polish slang](/blog/polish-slang) has more words in the same register if you want to understand what your Polish friends are texting each other.

There's a generational pattern here. *Hej* works for nearly anyone in a casual setting, including parents and grandparents writing to family. *Siema* and *elo* skew younger and can sound forced from an older speaker. *Czołem* tends to come from older men or from people being deliberately humorous.

## Witam and Witaj: Welcome, or a Little Presumptuous?

*Witam* (**VEE-tahm**) comes from the verb *witać*, "to welcome," so it literally means "I welcome (you)." The plural *witamy* (**vee-TAH-my**, "we welcome you") is everywhere on town signs, shop doors and museum walls. The informal *witaj* (**VEE-tie**) and plural *witajcie* (**vee-TIE-cheh**) are warmer and slightly literary, as in *Witaj w domu!* ("Welcome home!").

![Blue and yellow neon sign reading "Witamy w stolicy polskiej piosenki" (welcome to the capital of Polish song) at the Museum of Polish Song in Opole, a public way to say hello in Polish to visitors — Photo: Krzysztof Popławski, CC BY 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons](/blog/hello-in-polish-witamy-neon.webp)

The debate is about *witam* as an all-purpose hello in Polish, especially at the top of an email. Critics, including the writer and academic Michał Rusinek and the well-known linguist Jerzy Bralczyk, argue that welcoming belongs to the host: you welcome guests into your home, your event or your office. Opening an email to a stranger with *witam* can therefore sound as if the sender is placing themselves above the reader. Rusinek said publicly that he doesn't answer emails that start that way.

The other side points out that standard dictionaries treat *witam* simply as a greeting, without that hierarchy rule, and that plenty of Poles use it in emails without meaning anything by it. Both views are common, so the safe approach to this particular hello in Polish is simple:

- **In emails to people you don't know**, open with *Dzień dobry* or *Szanowna Pani / Szanowny Panie* (Dear Madam / Dear Sir).
- **When you are genuinely the host**, *witam* is perfect: *Witam Państwa serdecznie* (I warmly welcome you all) at the start of a talk or a party.
- **With friends**, *witaj* or *witajcie* is fine and sounds affectionate.

## Formal Hello in Polish: Pan, Pani and Państwo

Formal Polish replaces "you" with *Pan* (sir), *Pani* (madam) and *Państwo* (ladies and gentlemen, or a mixed group). These words shape how the conversation continues after *dzień dobry*.

| Polish | Sounds like | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Dzień dobry panu | jen DOH-bry PAH-noo | Good day to you (to a man) |
| Dzień dobry pani | jen DOH-bry PAH-nee | Good day to you (to a woman) |
| Dzień dobry Państwu | jen DOH-bry PAHN-stfoo | Good day to you all |
| Dzień dobry, panie Tomaszu | jen DOH-bry PAH-nyeh toh-MAH-shoo | Good day, Mr Tomasz (first name + Pan is common and warm) |
| Dzień dobry, pani profesor | jen DOH-bry PAH-nee proh-FEH-sor | Good day, Professor (to a woman) |

The switch from formal to informal is a small ritual of its own. The older or more senior person usually offers it, often with *Mówmy sobie po imieniu* (**MOOV-my SOH-byeh poh ee-MYEH-nyoo**, "let's use first names"). Until that happens, stay with *dzień dobry*. ## How to Say Hello in Polish to a Group

English speakers often say a single "hi" to a room. A group hello in Polish uses set phrases instead.

| Polish | Sounds like | When |
|---|---|---|
| Cześć wszystkim | cheshch FSHIST-keem | Hi everyone (friends, a class, an informal team) |
| Hej wszystkim | hey FSHIST-keem | Hey all (casual, common in messages) |
| Dzień dobry wszystkim | jen DOH-bry FSHIST-keem | Hello everyone (mixed or semi-formal groups) |
| Dzień dobry Państwu | jen DOH-bry PAHN-stfoo | Good day, ladies and gentlemen (formal) |
| Witam wszystkich | VEE-tahm FSHIST-keekh | Welcome, everyone (when you're the host or presenter) |
| Witajcie | vee-TIE-cheh | Welcome, all of you (warm, informal) |

*Cześć wszystkim* uses the "to everyone" form, while *witam wszystkich* uses the object form, because you're welcoming them. Just learn each one as a chunk.

## Saying Hello in Polish on the Phone

Phone greetings have their own conventions. When a private call comes in, most Poles answer with *Halo?* (**HAH-loh**) or *Słucham* (**SWOO-hahm**, literally "I'm listening"). *Tak, słucham?* ("Yes, I'm listening?") is also common. Some people consider *halo* slightly less elegant than *słucham*, but both are normal for personal calls.

The caller then gives the real greeting and says who they are: *Dzień dobry, mówi Anna Kowalska* (Good day, this is Anna Kowalska speaking) or, between friends, *Cześć, tu Kasia* (Hi, it's Kasia).

![Woman smiling while talking on her mobile phone at a wooden table, the moment to say hello in Polish with halo or słucham — Image by VinzentWeinbeer from Pixabay](/blog/hello-in-polish-phone-call.webp)

Business calls are different. The Polish government's [guide to telephone etiquette](https://zielonalinia.gov.pl/etykieta-rozmowy-telefonicznej/) recommends avoiding *tak*, *halo* and *słucham* at work and instead opening with the company name and your own name. If you ever answer a work phone in Poland, *Dzień dobry, [firma], mówi [imię]* is the pattern to copy.

## Handshakes, Cheek Kisses and the Threshold Rule

Words are only half of a hello in Polish; the other half is physical.

**Handshakes.** A firm handshake with eye contact is the standard way to greet someone new, in both business and social settings. In private life, the older person traditionally offers a hand to the younger one, and a woman to a man. At work, the more senior person goes first, regardless of gender. Between equals, whoever is quicker simply offers first.

**Cheek kisses.** Kisses on the cheek are for family and close friends, not new acquaintances. The traditional Polish pattern is three kisses on alternating cheeks, which you'll still meet at family gatherings and among older relatives. Many younger people, though, settle for one kiss or a hug, so let the other person lead and mirror what they do.

**Hand-kissing.** Some older Polish gentlemen still greet women by kissing their hand. It's a courtly, old-fashioned gesture rather than an expectation, and younger people don't do it. If it's offered to you and you'd rather not, simply extend your hand for a handshake.

**The threshold rule.** Never shake hands, hug or kiss someone across a doorway. Many Poles consider greeting over a threshold (*przez próg*) unlucky, and if you try, you'll likely hear a cheerful *Nie przez próg!* ("Not over the threshold!"). This isn't a fringe belief: in a 2018 survey, [CBOS found that 36% of Polish adults](https://cbos.pl/EN/publications/news/2018/24/newsletter.php) avoid shaking hands or hugging over a threshold. Step inside first, then greet.

## Religious and Regional Ways to Say Hello in Polish

You'll mostly hear the greetings above, but a couple of traditional ones are worth recognising, especially in the countryside, in the south and east, and among older or churchgoing Poles.

- **Szczęść Boże** (**shchenshch BOH-zheh**, "God bless"): a traditional greeting to people at work, especially farmers in the fields and miners. It's also used with priests. The usual replies are to repeat *Szczęść Boże* or answer *Daj Boże*.
- **Niech będzie pochwalony Jezus Chrystus** ("Praised be Jesus Christ"): a formal religious greeting, heard in church settings, from older people in villages, and when greeting priests or nuns. The reply is *Na wieki wieków, amen* ("For ever and ever, amen").

As a learner you don't need to use either, but recognising them makes a difference. If an elderly villager greets you with *Szczęść Boże*, a smile and a plain *dzień dobry*, the standard hello in Polish, is a perfectly polite reply.

## Common Mistakes When Saying Hello in Polish

Most errors with hello in Polish fall into a handful of patterns. Fix these and you'll sound more natural than many intermediate learners.

1. **Using *cześć* with strangers or elders.** It's the most noticeable slip. When in doubt, say *dzień dobry*.

2. **Saying *dobranoc* as a greeting.** It's only for leaving or going to bed. Arriving at 10 p.m., you still say *dobry wieczór*.

3. **Mangling *dzień*.** Three letters, *dzi*, make one soft "j" sound. Say "jen," not "dee-zen."

4. **Opening emails with *witam* to people you don't know.** Some readers won't mind; some will. *Dzień dobry* is safer.

5. **Staying silent when entering a small space.** In a waiting room or small shop, a quiet *dzień dobry* to the room is expected.

6. **Mixing registers.** *Cześć, proszę pana* ("hi, sir") or *Dzień dobry, stary* ("good day, dude") mash two worlds together. Keep the greeting and the form of address in the same register.

7. **Greeting across a doorway.** Step over the threshold first, then shake hands.

## Scenario: Saying Hello in Polish to a New Neighbour

You've just moved into a flat in Kraków. You knock on your neighbour's door with a small box of cookies. An older woman opens it. Here's how the exchange might go.

> **You:** *Dzień dobry! Jestem Tom, mieszkam pod siódemką.*
> (Good day! I'm Tom, I live at number seven.)
>
> **Neighbour:** *Dzień dobry, dzień dobry! Bardzo mi miło. Nie przez próg, proszę wejść!*
> (Hello, hello! Very nice to meet you. Not over the threshold, please come in!)
>
> **You (stepping inside, offering a hand):** *Bardzo mi miło. To dla pani.*
> (Very nice to meet you. This is for you.)
>
> **Neighbour:** *Ojej, dziękuję! Jestem Halina. Mówmy sobie po imieniu.*
> (Oh my, thank you! I'm Halina. Let's use first names.)
>
> **You:** *Z przyjemnością. Cześć, Halino!*
> (With pleasure. Hi, Halina!)

Notice the order. You start with *dzień dobry*, she invites you over the threshold before shaking hands, and only after she offers first names do you switch to *cześć*. That sequence is the cultural logic of Polish greetings in miniature.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common way to say hello in Polish?

It depends on the relationship. With strangers and in public, *dzień dobry* (jen DOH-bry) is by far the most common. Among friends and family, *cześć* (cheshch) and *hej* (hey) dominate. If you can only learn one, learn *dzień dobry*, because it's never rude.

### How do you say hello in Polish phonetically?

Phonetically, hello in Polish is **cheshch** (*cześć*) or **jen DOH-bry** (*dzień dobry*); *dobry wieczór* sounds like **DOH-bry VYEH-choor**, and *hej* like the English **hey**. Polish stress almost always falls on the second-to-last syllable, so stress *DOH* in *dobry* and *VYEH* in *wieczór*.

### Is cześć a rude way to say hello in Polish?

No, but it's informal. Saying *cześć* to a friend is completely normal. Saying it to an elderly stranger, a shop assistant or your professor can come across as overfamiliar, a bit like greeting your bank manager with "hiya."

### How do you say good morning in Polish?

Use *dzień dobry*. Polish doesn't have a separate everyday phrase for good morning, so the same greeting covers morning and afternoon. With a partner or child you might say *Dzień dobry, kochanie*, and casually you'll sometimes hear just *Dobry!*

### How do you say hello in Polish to a girl?

Exactly as you would to anyone else: *cześć* or *hej* if you're on first-name terms, *dzień dobry* if not. Polish greetings don't change with gender. If you add her name, use its calling form, for example *Cześć, Kasiu!* or *Hej, Asiu!*

### Can I start an email with "Witam"?

You can, and many Poles do, but some readers see it as presumptuous because *witam* traditionally belongs to the host. For a first email to someone you don't know, *Dzień dobry* or *Szanowna Pani / Szanowny Panie* is the safer opening.

### How do you say hello in Polish formally?

Say *dzień dobry* (jen DOH-bry), or *dobry wieczór* in the evening, and address the person as *Pan* (to a man) or *Pani* (to a woman). For a group, *Dzień dobry Państwu* is the most formal option. In a first email, the formal hello in Polish is *Szanowna Pani* or *Szanowny Panie*.

### How do Poles answer the phone?

With *Halo?*, *Słucham* or *Tak, słucham?* on personal calls. The caller then greets properly and introduces themselves. At work, people usually answer with the company name and their own name instead.

Hello in Polish is the first thing you'll say every single day, which makes them the fastest place to start sounding natural. Say *dzień dobry* to the next room you walk into, even if it's only in your head, and keep *cześć* ready for the moment someone says *mów mi po imieniu*. Once a hello in Polish comes out without thinking, the rest of the conversation gets noticeably easier.
