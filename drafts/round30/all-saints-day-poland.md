On All Saints Day in Poland, cemeteries turn into seas of light. On 1 November, Poles across the country travel to the graves of their parents, grandparents and friends, clean them, cover them in chrysanthemums and light small glass candles called *znicze*. By evening, whole hillsides glow red and gold, and the mood is quiet, warm and surprisingly social.

For visitors, All Saints Day in Poland can be one of the most moving things to witness in the country. It is not a sad day in the way a funeral is sad, and it is nothing like a costume party. It is a family reunion that happens partly at the dinner table and partly at the cemetery gate.

This guide is written with next year's season in mind: All Saints Day 2027 falls on Monday, 1 November, and Zaduszki on Tuesday, 2 November. Below you will find the difference between the two days, what znicze and chrysanthemums mean, the famous collection at Warsaw's Powązki cemetery, the pre-Christian roots of the holiday, a jazz tradition you may not expect, and the Polish phrases and etiquette that help you join in respectfully.

## What Is Wszystkich Świętych?

In Polish, All Saints Day is **Dzień Wszystkich Świętych** ("the Day of All Saints"), or in church language **Uroczystość Wszystkich Świętych** ("the Solemnity of All Saints"). In conversation, people simply say **Wszystkich Świętych**. Grammatically, that is a genitive plural (*of all the saints*), with the word *dzień* ("day") left out.

It is a Catholic feast honoring all saints, known and unknown. The date goes back to the early medieval Church in Rome, and by the 9th century 1 November was an established holy day in much of Western Europe.

In practice, though, All Saints Day in Poland today is less about saints and more about family. People think of their own dead, which is why many Poles loosely call the whole period **Święto Zmarłych** ("the Day of the Dead"), even though that name strictly belongs to the following day.

1 November is a public holiday. Schools and offices close, and large shops are shut under Poland's trading rules for public holidays.

## Wszystkich Świętych vs Zaduszki: 1 and 2 November

The two days at the heart of the All Saints Day season in Poland are closely linked, but they are not the same.

### 1 November: All Saints Day

On All Saints Day, Poland stops. It is the official day off, so it is the day most families travel to the cemetery. There are masses, often held at cemetery chapels or in the open air, and the paths between graves fill with people from morning until late at night.

### 2 November: Zaduszki

The next day is **Dzień Zaduszny**, usually called **Zaduszki**. This is All Souls' Day, when the Catholic Church prays for all the faithful departed. The custom of setting aside 2 November for prayers for the dead goes back to the year 998, when Odilo, abbot of the monastery of Cluny in France, introduced it for his monks.

Zaduszki is not a public holiday in Poland. After All Saints Day, Poland goes back to work, but many people visit the cemetery again after work, light another candle, or attend an evening mass. In churches, priests read **wypominki**, long lists of names of the dead submitted by parishioners, a custom recorded in Poland since at least the 15th century.

### Why the difference matters

Because only 1 November is a day off, it has absorbed most of the customs that technically belong to Zaduszki. If you want to see All Saints Day in Poland at its busiest, go on the 1st. If you prefer quieter cemeteries with the candles still burning, go on the evening of the 2nd.

## How All Saints Day in Poland Begins: Cleaning the Graves

Preparations for All Saints Day in Poland start days, sometimes weeks, in advance.

Families visit the cemetery to tidy their graves. They sweep away fallen leaves, wash the stone, scrub away moss and remove last year's burned-out candles. In Polish this is **sprzątanie grobów** ("cleaning the graves").

By the morning of All Saints Day in Poland, cemeteries look freshly scrubbed. Then the second wave arrives: flowers, candles and family members who have traveled from other cities.

### On the road

Around All Saints Day in Poland, the roads are among the busiest of the year. Adult children drive back to their hometowns, sometimes visiting cemeteries in several places in one long day. Every year the police run an operation called **Akcja Znicz** ("Operation Candle"), with extra patrols on main roads and traffic management around large cemeteries.

In 2027, 1 November falls on a Monday, which creates a long weekend from Saturday 30 October. Expect busy roads and full trains, and if you are driving in a city, watch for detours near cemeteries. Many cities also run extra or special public transport lines to their largest cemeteries.

## Znicze: The Candles That Light Up the Night

The single most recognizable symbol of the holiday is the **znicz** (plural **znicze**). A znicz is a candle in a glass jar, usually with a metal lid that has holes to protect the flame from wind and rain. They come in red, white, amber and many other colors, from small cheap jars to large decorative lanterns that burn for days.

The custom of lighting fires on graves is older than Christianity. Slavic peoples lit fires to guide the souls of the dead, and the Church later gave the flame a Christian meaning as a symbol of eternal light. The word *znicz* itself also suggests something ancient: it is often used in Polish for a sacred or eternal flame.

Families bring several znicze, place them on the grave, light them and let them burn. Before All Saints Day in Poland, supermarkets and kiosks stack them in huge piles, and stalls outside cemetery gates sell them right up to the last minute.

### The night glow

The most unforgettable sight of All Saints Day in Poland comes after dark. Every grave holds several flames, and large cemeteries turn into fields of flickering light that you can see from a distance.

If you are visiting Poland, a walk through a cemetery on the evening of 1 November is the best way to understand why All Saints Day in Poland matters so much. Go quietly, keep to the paths, and you will be welcome.

### Candles for forgotten graves

Not every grave has a family left to care for it. Some people light an extra znicz on a neglected grave nearby, so that no grave stays completely dark. Old cemeteries, especially, are full of graves whose families emigrated, died out or were scattered by war.

Around All Saints Day, Poland also remembers its soldiers. Poland's Institute of National Remembrance (IPN) has repeatedly appealed to the public to light candles on the graves of soldiers, partisans and other independence fighters, from the 19th-century uprisings through both world wars to the Solidarity era. Ten days later, the same candles reappear at monuments for [Polish Independence Day](/blog/polish-independence-day), which makes early November a single long season of remembrance.

## Chrysanthemums: The Flower of 1 November

Next to the candles stand the flowers, and for All Saints Day in Poland that means **chryzantemy** (chrysanthemums). Pots of white, yellow, orange, purple and deep red chrysanthemums are sold everywhere in late October, from markets and garden centers to stalls outside the cemetery gate.

They are popular for a practical reason: they bloom in autumn and survive frost far better than cut flowers. A big potted chrysanthemum can last on a grave for weeks.

The association is so strong that many Poles would not give chrysanthemums as a gift, because they feel like cemetery flowers. If you are invited to a Polish home, choose another bouquet.

## Powązki and the Kwesta for Old Graves

On All Saints Day, Poland's most famous cemetery, Warsaw's **Stare Powązki**, draws large crowds. The cemetery was founded in 1790 and covers about 43 hectares. It is the resting place of composer Stanisław Moniuszko, Nobel Prize-winning novelist Władysław Reymont, novelist Bolesław Prus and many other Polish figures, and its Avenue of the Distinguished (*Aleja Zasłużonych*) dates from 1925. You can read more about its history on [Wikipedia's page on Powązki Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powązki_Cemetery).

Do not confuse it with the nearby **Powązki Military Cemetery** (*Cmentarz Wojskowy na Powązkach*), a separate and newer cemetery. Many soldiers and victims of the communist era are buried there, including in a section known as *Łączka*, where the communist security services secretly buried people they executed.

### How the kwesta began

Every year around All Saints Day, Poland's capital sees volunteers standing at the gates and along the paths of Stare Powązki holding sealed collection tins. This is the **kwesta**, a public collection for the restoration of the cemetery's historic tombstones.

It began in 1975 on the initiative of Jerzy Waldorff, a music critic who led a civic committee to save the neglected cemetery. He was inspired by a charity collection scene in Prus's novel *Lalka* (*The Doll*). Fittingly, Prus himself is buried at Powązki.

### Who collects and what it pays for

The collectors are a mix of actors, journalists, scouts, high school students and other volunteers. Some have taken part for decades: actress Maja Komorowska collected at the first kwesta in 1975 and was still collecting at the Main Gate in 2023.

The money pays for conservation work on old monuments, chapels and gravestones. According to coverage of the 2023 collection, roughly 1,600 conservation works had been carried out over the years. The kwesta ran every year until the pandemic forced it online in 2020, and it has since returned to the cemetery gates.

## From Dziady to Zaduszki: The Pre-Christian Roots

Long before Christianity reached Poland, Slavic peoples held rites for the dead called **dziady** ("forefathers"). The word meant both the spirits of the ancestors and the feasts held for them.

### How the old rites worked

According to ethnographic accounts, there were dziady in spring, around the start of May, and in autumn, from the night of 31 October into 1 November, leading to a day of the dead around 2 November. Families "fed" the souls with honey, groats, eggs and other foods left on tables, on the floor or on graves. Fires were lit to show wandering souls the way.

Wandering beggars, also called *dziady*, played a role too: people gave them food or money, and in return they prayed for the family's dead. The Church opposed many of these practices, but it also absorbed them, and the dates lined up neatly with All Saints and All Souls.

Traces survived for centuries. Polish folk custom long included leaving food and drink at the threshold for the dead on the night of 1 to 2 November, and avoiding certain household chores that night so as not to harm visiting spirits. If you enjoy this side of Polish culture, our guide to [Polish folklore creatures](/blog/polish-folklore-creatures) introduces the spirits and demons that Slavic villagers believed shared their world.

### Mickiewicz's Dziady

The old rite is also the backbone of one of the most important works in Polish literature. Adam Mickiewicz's verse drama **Dziady** (usually translated as *Forefathers' Eve*) was inspired by these village ceremonies, though he blended the folklore with his own Romantic vision. Parts II and IV were published in 1823, and Part III, the famous patriotic section, in 1832.

Part II stages the rite itself. Villagers summon the dead so that their souls can find peace, and a procession of ghosts appears: two children who cannot enter heaven because they never suffered, a cruel lord tormented by birds, and a young shepherdess, Zosia, who never returned anyone's love. Each teaches a moral lesson.

The drama never lost its political edge. A ban on performing it in Warsaw became part of Poland's 1968 political crisis. Mickiewicz is best known abroad for his epic poem, covered in our article on [Adam Mickiewicz and Pan Tadeusz](/blog/adam-mickiewicz-pan-tadeusz), but at this time of year it is *Dziady* that feels closest to home.

## Zaduszki Jazzowe: Jazz for All Souls

One of the most unusual All Saints Day traditions in Poland comes from the world of jazz. **Zaduszki Jazzowe** ("Jazz All Souls") are jazz concerts held in the days around 1 and 2 November.

The tradition began in Kraków on 1 November 1954, when musicians gathered in a primary school gym on ulica Królowej Jadwigi for a jam session on their only free day. From 1956, a jazz club called Helikon organized the Kraków concerts, which are considered the oldest series of their kind.

Today Zaduszki Jazzowe take place in many Polish cities, including Warsaw, Poznań, Wrocław, Lublin, Gdańsk and Rzeszów. In many places, Dominican friars host them. There has even been an edition in Toronto since 2006. On the evening of All Saints Day in Poland, jazz clubs and church cellars are a good alternative to staying in.

## All Saints Day in Poland vs Halloween

Halloween falls on 31 October, the night before the feast Poland marks so solemnly, and you may see pumpkins and costumes in Polish shops and bars. The two holidays could hardly be more different in mood, though.

Halloween is about fun and fright. Wszystkich Świętych is calm, reflective and focused on family. Many families spend 31 October preparing for the next day, buying candles and flowers, cooking for visiting relatives and planning the cemetery route. If a Polish family invites you to spend All Saints Day the Polish way, at the cemetery and the dinner table, treat it as you would a family gathering after a memorial, not as a party.

## All Saints Day in Poland: Etiquette for Visitors

On All Saints Day in Poland, cemeteries are open to everyone, and tourists are welcome. A few simple habits will help you fit in.

- **Dress neatly and warmly.** There is no strict dress code, but many people wear dark or muted colors. Bring a hat and gloves, because you will be outside for a long time.
- **Keep your voice low.** People chat quietly with relatives, but loud laughter or phone calls feel out of place.
- **Do not say "happy holiday".** Polish greetings like *Wesołych Świąt* belong to Christmas and Easter. On 1 November, nobody wishes anyone a happy anything.
- **Leave candles and flowers alone.** Never move or take items from a grave, and never step on graves themselves.
- **Photograph with care.** Wide shots of the glowing cemetery are fine; close-ups of people praying at a family grave are not.
- **Carry coins.** If you visit Powązki or another cemetery with a kwesta, a small donation is a lovely gesture.
- **Light a candle if you like.** Buying a znicz at the gate and placing it on a neglected grave, or at a war memorial, is a respectful way to take part.

### Prayers and phrases you will hear

The most common Polish prayer for the dead is the traditional *Requiem*:

> *Wieczny odpoczynek racz im dać, Panie,*
> *a światłość wiekuista niechaj im świeci.*

In plain English: "Grant them eternal rest, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them." You will also hear an older version that begins *Wieczne odpoczywanie*.

When someone mentions a person who has died, Poles often add **Świeć, Panie, nad jego duszą** ("Lord, shine upon his soul"), or *nad jej duszą* for a woman. It is similar to "may he rest in peace" in English. Use it only if you are comfortable with its religious tone.

To talk about whose grave you are visiting, you will need family words in the genitive: *grób babci* (grandma's grave), *grób dziadka* (grandpa's grave), *grób rodziców* (my parents' grave). Our family lesson is a good place to learn them:

## Scenario: Visiting Grandma's Grave

Kasia is showing her English friend Mark what All Saints Day in Poland really means: a trip to her grandmother's grave.

**Kasia:** Jutro jedziemy na cmentarz do babci.
*(Tomorrow we're going to the cemetery to visit grandma.)*

**Mark:** Co mam kupić?
*(What should I buy?)*

**Kasia:** Kup dwa znicze i chryzantemę.
*(Buy two grave candles and a chrysanthemum.)*

**Mark:** Białą czy żółtą?
*(A white one or a yellow one?)*

**Kasia:** Białą. Babcia lubiła białe kwiaty.
*(White. Grandma liked white flowers.)*

At the cemetery, Mark notices a dark corner.

**Mark:** Tu jest dużo grobów bez zniczy.
*(There are a lot of graves here without candles.)*

**Kasia:** Zapalmy jeden znicz na tamtym starym grobie.
*(Let's light one candle on that old grave over there.)*

**Mark:** Dobry pomysł.
*(Good idea.)*

Notice the endings: *dwa znicze* (plural after the number two), *bez zniczy* (genitive plural after *bez*, "without"), and *na tamtym starym grobie* (locative after *na*). Even a quiet cemetery visit is full of Polish cases.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is All Saints Day a public holiday in Poland?

Yes. On All Saints Day, Poland observes a statutory public holiday on 1 November (Wszystkich Świętych), and large shops are closed. 2 November (Zaduszki) is a normal working day, although many people visit cemeteries again after work.

### What is the difference between Wszystkich Świętych and Zaduszki?

Wszystkich Świętych, on 1 November, is the feast of all saints. Zaduszki, on 2 November, is All Souls' Day, dedicated to praying for all the dead. In practice, Poles visit family graves mainly on 1 November because it is the day off.

### What are znicze?

Znicze are candles in glass jars, usually with a metal lid, made to burn outdoors on graves. On All Saints Day in Poland, families light them and leave them burning, which creates the famous glow over Polish cemeteries at night.

### Why do Poles put chrysanthemums on graves?

Chrysanthemums bloom in autumn and tolerate cold weather, so they last for weeks on a grave. They have become so closely tied to 1 November that many Poles see them as cemetery flowers.

### Can tourists visit Polish cemeteries on All Saints Day?

Yes. Visitors are part of All Saints Day in Poland, and walking through a cemetery on the evening of 1 November is unforgettable. Keep your voice down, stay on the paths and do not touch candles or flowers on graves.

### When is All Saints Day 2027 in Poland?

In 2027, All Saints Day in Poland falls on Monday, 1 November, which creates a long weekend from Saturday 30 October. Zaduszki is on Tuesday, 2 November 2027.

Whether you light a znicz for your own family or simply walk among the flames on a cold November evening, All Saints Day in Poland is a reminder that remembering the dead can bring the living closer together.
