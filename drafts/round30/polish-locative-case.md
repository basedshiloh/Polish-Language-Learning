The Polish locative case is the only case that never stands on its own: every locative noun comes straight after a preposition, almost always *w*, *na*, *o*, *przy* or *po*. You will never see a locative form floating alone at the start of a sentence or acting as a subject. Spot one of those five prepositions with a "where" or "about" meaning, and the locative is usually next.

That makes the Polish locative case one of the friendliest cases to recognise and one of the most useful to master early. You need it to say where you live (*w Warszawie*), where something is (*na stole*), what you are talking about (*o filmie*) and what you do after work (*po pracy*). The catch is the endings. They come with consonant changes that turn *stół* into *stole*, *ręka* into *ręce* and *miasto* into *mieście*.

This guide walks through the Polish locative case step by step: the question words, the singular endings for each gender with every common consonant alternation, the simple plural, adjectives, personal pronouns, city names, the most common mistakes and a practice set with answers.

## What Is the Polish Locative Case (Miejscownik)?

In Polish, the locative is called **miejscownik**, from *miejsce* (place). The name tells you its main job: describing location. In grammar books it is usually listed as the sixth of the seven Polish cases, and it is the one case in the system that is only ever used with a preposition.

The Polish locative case answers these questions:

| Question | English | Example answer |
|---|---|---|
| O kim? | About whom? | O Marku. (About Marek.) |
| O czym? | About what? | O wakacjach. (About the holidays.) |
| Gdzie? (with w / na) | Where? | W pracy. (At work.) |
| Na czym? / W czym? | On what? / In what? | Na stole. (On the table.) |

The PolishPal A0 course focuses first on the nominative, accusative, instrumental and genitive, so the locative builds on what you already know. If you have read our [overview of the Polish cases](/blog/polish-cases-overview), think of the locative as the "where and about" case that completes the everyday set.

## The Five Prepositions That Take the Polish Locative Case

Only five common prepositions take the Polish locative case. Learn them with their meanings and you will know exactly when the locative is coming.

| Preposition | Meaning with the locative | Example |
|---|---|---|
| w | in, at | Mieszkam w Gdańsku. (I live in Gdańsk.) |
| na | on, at | Kot śpi na kanapie. (The cat sleeps on the sofa.) |
| o | about; at (clock time) | Rozmawiamy o filmie. (We're talking about the film.) / o ósmej (at eight) |
| przy | by, at, next to | Siedzimy przy stole. (We're sitting at the table.) |
| po | after; around, across | Po obiedzie idę na spacer. (After lunch I go for a walk.) / Spacerujemy po parku. (We walk around the park.) |

Watch out: *w*, *na*, *o* and *po* can also take the accusative, with a different meaning. *Jestem na poczcie* (locative) means "I'm at the post office", but *Idę na pocztę* (accusative) means "I'm going to the post office". Our guide to [Polish prepositions](/blog/polish-prepositions) explains this motion-versus-location rule in detail, with a full table of which case every common preposition takes.

### O + Locative for Clock Time

The preposition *o* also uses the Polish locative case for "at" a clock time: *o pierwszej, o drugiej, o piątej, o dwunastej*. The hour is a feminine ordinal number, so it takes the feminine adjective ending *-ej*. The daily routine lesson practises exactly this pattern.

## Polish Locative Case Endings: Masculine Nouns

Polish locative case endings for singular nouns come down to one big choice: **-e** (with a consonant change) or **-u**. The deciding factor is the last consonant of the stem.

### Masculine Hard Stems Take -e (With a Consonant Change)

Most masculine nouns ending in a "hard" consonant take *-e*, often written *-ie*, and the consonant before it softens. This is the part of the Polish locative case that needs the most practice.

| Nominative | Locative | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| sklep (shop) | w sklepie | p → pie |
| autobus (bus) | w autobusie | s → sie |
| Londyn (London) | w Londynie | n → nie |
| kot (cat) | o kocie | t → cie |
| student (student) | o studencie | t → cie |
| samochód (car) | w samochodzie | ó → o, d → dzie |
| teatr (theatre) | w teatrze | r → rze |
| stół (table) | na stole | ó → o, ł → le |
| kościół (church) | w kościele | ó → o, ł → le |
| Kraków | w Krakowie | ó → o |
| obiad (lunch) | po obiedzie | a → e, d → dzie |
| świat (world) | na świecie | a → e, t → cie |
| las (forest) | w lesie | a → e, s → sie |
| pies (dog) | o psie | e drops out, s → sie |

Two kinds of change happen here. The **consonant** softens (*t → ci, d → dzi, r → rz, ł → l*), and sometimes the **vowel** shifts too (*ó → o* in *stół → stole*, *a → e* in *las → lesie*). You do not need to memorise these as separate rules: the same alternations appear again in feminine and neuter nouns.

### Masculine Stems in K, G, Ch Take -u

If the stem ends in *k*, *g* or *ch*, the ending is *-u* and the consonant stays as it is (a "mobile" *e* can still drop out, as in *rynek → rynku*):

- park → w parku (in the park)
- Gdańsk → w Gdańsku (in Gdańsk)
- pociąg → w pociągu (on the train)
- rynek → na rynku (in the market square)
- człowiek → o człowieku (about a person)
- Bóg → o Bogu (about God)

### Masculine Soft Stems Take -u

Soft stems (ending in *ń, ś, ć, ź, dź, j, l*) and the "hardened" consonants *c, cz, sz, ż, rz* also take *-u*:

- hotel → w hotelu (in the hotel)
- pokój → w pokoju (in the room)
- kraj → w kraju (in the country)
- koń → na koniu (on a horse)
- Poznań → w Poznaniu (in Poznań)
- Paryż → w Paryżu (in Paris)
- lekarz → o lekarzu (about the doctor)
- miesiąc → w miesiącu (in a month)
- dworzec → na dworcu (at the station)

### Three Famous Exceptions: Dom, Syn, Pan

Three very common hard-stem nouns break the rule and take *-u* instead of *-e*:

- dom → **w domu** (at home)
- syn → **o synu** (about my son)
- pan → **o panu** (about the gentleman / about you, sir)

A few more irregular nouns are worth learning as whole words: *tydzień → w tygodniu* (in the week), *dzień → w dniu* (on the day), *ojciec → o ojcu* (about Father) and *mąż → o mężu* (about my husband).

### Masculine Nouns Ending in -a

Masculine nouns that end in *-a*, such as *tata*, *kolega* and *mężczyzna*, take feminine-style endings in the Polish locative case: *o tacie* (about Dad), *o koledze* (about a friend), *o mężczyźnie* (about a man).

## Polish Locative Case Endings: Neuter Nouns

Neuter nouns follow the same logic as masculine ones in the Polish locative case.

| Stem type | Ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Hard stem | -e (with change) | miasto → w mieście, okno → w oknie, kino → w kinie, biuro → w biurze, lato → w lecie, auto → w aucie, drzewo → na drzewie |
| Stem in k, g, ch | -u | lotnisko → na lotnisku, jabłko → o jabłku, mleko → w mleku, łóżko → w łóżku, oko → w oku, dziecko → o dziecku |
| Soft stem (-e, -ie, -ę) | -u | morze → w morzu, pole → na polu, mieszkanie → w mieszkaniu, śniadanie → po śniadaniu, imię → o imieniu |
| Ending in -um | no change | muzeum → w muzeum, centrum → w centrum |

The change in *miasto → mieście* combines both kinds of alternation: the vowel shifts (*a → e*) and *st* becomes *ści*. It is one of the most frequent locative forms in Polish, because *w mieście* simply means "in town".

## Polish Locative Case Endings: Feminine Nouns (-e, -i, -y)

Feminine nouns in the Polish locative case take one of three endings: *-e*, *-i* or *-y*.

### Hard Stems Take -e

Feminine nouns ending in *-a* after a hard consonant take *-e*, with the same consonant changes you met above:

| Nominative | Locative | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| kawa (coffee) | o kawie | w → wie |
| mama (Mum) | o mamie | m → mie |
| Warszawa | w Warszawie | w → wie |
| szkoła (school) | w szkole | ł → le |
| woda (water) | w wodzie | d → dzie |
| gazeta (newspaper) | w gazecie | t → cie |
| siostra (sister) | o siostrze | r → rze |
| gwiazda (star) | o gwieździe | a → e, zd → ździe |

### K, G, Ch Change Before -e

Here is where feminine nouns surprise learners. Before the locative *-e*, the consonants *k*, *g* and *ch* change into *c*, *dz* and *sz*:

| Change | Nominative | Locative |
|---|---|---|
| k → c | ręka (hand) | w ręce |
| k → c | książka (book) | w książce |
| k → c | Polska (Poland) | w Polsce |
| k → c | apteka (pharmacy) | w aptece |
| g → dz | noga (leg) | na nodze |
| g → dz | droga (road) | w drodze |
| g → dz | Praga (Prague) | w Pradze |
| ch → sz | mucha (fly) | o musze |

This is why "in Poland" is *w Polsce*, not *w Polskie*. Once you know *k → c*, the form makes sense. You will also hear *w ręku* (in hand) in set phrases, an old form that Polish still uses alongside *w ręce*.

### Soft Stems Take -i

Feminine nouns with a soft stem take *-i*. This includes nouns in *-ia*, *-ja* and *-nia*, and feminine nouns ending in a soft consonant:

- kuchnia → w kuchni (in the kitchen)
- babcia → o babci (about Grandma)
- restauracja → w restauracji (in the restaurant)
- Francja → we Francji (in France)
- Anglia → w Anglii (in England)
- Gdynia → w Gdyni (in Gdynia)
- miłość → o miłości (about love)
- wieś → na wsi (in the countryside)
- Łódź → w Łodzi (in Łódź)

### C, Cz, Sz, Ż, Rz Stems Take -y

After the "hardened" consonants *c, cz, sz, ż* and *rz*, Polish spelling does not allow *-i*, so the ending is *-y*:

- ulica → na ulicy (on the street)
- praca → w pracy (at work)
- noc → w nocy (at night)
- plaża → na plaży (on the beach)
- Bydgoszcz → w Bydgoszczy (in Bydgoszcz)

A useful shortcut: for feminine nouns, the locative singular is identical to the dative singular. *Mamie* means both "about Mum" (*o mamie*) and "to Mum" (*mamie*, as in *daję mamie kwiaty*). Learn one and you know the other.

## The Plural: -ach for Every Gender

After all those singular endings, the plural is a relief. In the Polish locative case, almost every plural noun of every gender ends in **-ach**:

| Singular | Plural locative |
|---|---|
| dom (house) | w domach |
| sklep (shop) | w sklepach |
| kot (cat) | o kotach |
| miasto (city) | w miastach |
| góra (mountain) | w górach |
| ręka (hand) | w rękach |
| oko (eye) | w oczach |
| dziecko (child) | o dzieciach |
| człowiek (person) | o ludziach |

The stem may change in the plural (*dziecko → dzieci → dzieciach*, *człowiek → ludzie → ludziach*), but the ending stays *-ach*. Plural-only place names follow the same rule: *Tatry → w Tatrach*, *Katowice → w Katowicach*, *Kielce → w Kielcach*.

The main exceptions are three country names that keep an old *-ech* ending: *Niemcy → w Niemczech* (in Germany), *Włochy → we Włoszech* (in Italy) and *Węgry → na Węgrzech* (in Hungary).

## Adjectives in the Polish Locative Case

Adjectives, possessives and demonstratives agree with the noun in the Polish locative case. There are only three sets of endings to learn:

| Noun type | Adjective ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine and neuter singular | -ym / -im | w dużym mieście, w nowym domu, w polskim sklepie, w drogim hotelu |
| Feminine singular | -ej | w dużej kuchni, w polskiej szkole, na tej ulicy |
| All plurals | -ych / -ich | w dużych miastach, o polskich filmach, w tych sklepach |

Use *-im* and *-ich* after *k*, *g* and soft stems (*polski → w polskim*, *drogi → w drogim*), and *-ym* and *-ych* everywhere else.

The same pattern covers the small words you use all the time:

| Word | Masc. / neut. | Feminine | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| ten / ta / to (this) | tym | tej | tych |
| mój / moja / moje (my) | moim | mojej | moich |
| twój / twoja / twoje (your) | twoim | twojej | twoich |
| nasz / nasza / nasze (our) | naszym | naszej | naszych |

Examples: *w tym domu* (in this house), *o mojej siostrze* (about my sister), *w naszym mieście* (in our town), *w moich rękach* (in my hands).

Tip: the masculine and neuter singular adjective endings *-ym / -im* are the same as in the instrumental. *W dużym mieście* (locative) and *z dużym psem* (instrumental) use an identical adjective form, so if you have learned one, you already have the other.

## Personal Pronouns in the Polish Locative Case

Personal pronouns in the Polish locative case are short and very frequent, because people talk about each other all the time.

| Nominative | Locative | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ja (I) | o mnie | Mówisz o mnie? (Are you talking about me?) |
| ty (you) | o tobie | Myślę o tobie. (I'm thinking about you.) |
| on (he) | o nim | Co o nim wiesz? (What do you know about him?) |
| ona (she) | o niej | Rozmawiamy o niej. (We're talking about her.) |
| ono (it) | o nim | o nim (about it, neuter) |
| my (we) | o nas | Nie zapomnij o nas. (Don't forget about us.) |
| wy (you, plural) | o was | Słyszałem o was. (I've heard about you.) |
| oni / one (they) | o nich | Nie wiem nic o nich. (I know nothing about them.) |
| pan / pani (formal you) | o panu / o pani | Dużo o panu słyszałem. (I've heard a lot about you, sir.) |
| państwo (formal you, plural) | o państwu | o państwu (about you, ladies and gentlemen) |
| siebie (oneself) | o sobie | Opowiedz mi o sobie. (Tell me about yourself.) |

Notice the *n-* in *nim, niej, nich*. Third-person pronouns always take it after a preposition, which in the locative means always. *Opowiedz mi o sobie* is worth memorising as a whole phrase: you will hear it at job interviews, language exchanges and first dates.

## Common Mistakes With the Polish Locative Case

These are some of the most typical errors English speakers make with the Polish locative case.

### Using the Locative for Movement

The Polish locative case describes where something **is**, not where it is going. *Jestem w szkole* (I'm at school) is locative, but "I'm going to school" is *Idę do szkoły*, with the genitive. Likewise *Jestem na poczcie* but *Idę na pocztę*.

### Forgetting the Consonant Change

Learners often add *-e* without softening the consonant, a very common slip with the Polish locative case. It is *w mieście*, not *w miastie*; *na stole*, not *na stołe*; *w Polsce*, not *w Polskie*; *na nodze*, not *na nogie*.

### Adding -e After K, G, Ch in Masculine Nouns

Masculine nouns in *k*, *g* and *ch* take *-u*: *w parku*, *w Gdańsku*, *w pociągu*. Forms such as *w parkie* do not exist.

### Forgetting Dom, Syn and Pan

The standard forms are *w domu*, *o synu* and *o panu*. Because *dom* is so frequent, *w domu* is the one to drill first.

### Mixing Up Nad Morzem and W Morzu

*Nad* and *pod* take the instrumental for position, not the locative: *nad morzem* (by the sea), *pod stołem* (under the table). The locative appears with *w*: *w morzu* (in the sea).

### Confusing O Nim With O Niego

*O* takes the locative when it means "about": *Mówię o nim* (I'm talking about him). Some verbs, however, use *o* with the accusative: *Martwię się o niego* (I'm worried about him). Learn these verbs as fixed pairs.

## Scenario: Talking About Where You Live

Here is a short conversation full of the Polish locative case, the kind you might have at a language exchange.

> **Ola:** Gdzie mieszkasz?
> *(Where do you live?)*
>
> **Tom:** Mieszkam w Krakowie, w małym mieszkaniu w centrum.
> *(I live in Kraków, in a small flat in the centre.)*
>
> **Ola:** A pracujesz w Krakowie?
> *(And do you work in Kraków?)*
>
> **Tom:** Nie, pracuję w Katowicach. W pociągu zawsze czytam.
> *(No, I work in Katowice. I always read on the train.)*
>
> **Ola:** O czym teraz czytasz?
> *(What are you reading about at the moment?)*
>
> **Tom:** O historii Polski. A ty? Co robisz po pracy?
> *(About Polish history. And you? What do you do after work?)*
>
> **Ola:** Po pracy odpoczywam w ogrodzie. Mam mały dom pod Krakowem.
> *(After work I relax in the garden. I have a small house near Kraków.)*

Count the locatives: *w Krakowie, w mieszkaniu, w centrum, w Katowicach, w pociągu, o czym, o historii, po pracy, w ogrodzie*. Notice also *pod Krakowem*: *pod* means "near" here and takes the instrumental, not the locative.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the Polish locative case called in Polish?

It is called *miejscownik*, from *miejsce* (place). Polish school grammar lists it with the question words *o kim? o czym?* (about whom? about what?).

### Can the Polish locative case be used without a preposition?

No. In modern Polish the locative always follows a preposition, usually *w, na, o, przy* or *po*. That is why spotting one of those prepositions is the quickest way to recognise it.

### How do I know whether a noun takes -e or -u?

Look at the last consonant of the stem. Masculine and neuter hard stems usually take *-e* with a consonant change (*kot → kocie*). Stems in *k, g, ch* and soft stems take *-u* (*park → parku*, *hotel → hotelu*). *Dom, syn* and *pan* are the main exceptions.

### Is the locative the same as the dative?

For feminine singular nouns, yes: *o mamie* (locative) and *mamie* (dative) look identical. For masculine nouns they usually differ: *o kocie* (locative) but *kotu* (dative).

### Why is it "w Polsce" and not "w Polskie"?

Because *k* changes to *c* before the feminine locative *-e*. The same change gives *w ręce*, *w książce* and *w Ameryce*.

Once the Polish locative case clicks, you can describe where you live, where you work, where things are and what you are talking about, which covers a huge share of everyday conversation. Start with *w domu, w pracy, w Polsce* and *o mnie*, and add new nouns to that frame as you meet them.
