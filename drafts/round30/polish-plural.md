The Polish plural looks chaotic at first: *kot* becomes *koty*, *książka* becomes *książki*, *okno* becomes *okna*, and then *student* turns into *studenci* and *Polak* into *Polacy*. But the system is far more regular than it seems. Once you know which of two plural "genders" a noun belongs to and what its stem ends in, most forms are predictable.

The key idea that unlocks the Polish plural is this: in the singular Polish has three genders, but in the plural it only has two. One group is nouns for **men** (the masculine-personal plural). The other group is **everything else**: women, children, animals, objects and ideas. That split affects nouns, adjectives, pronouns and past-tense verbs.

This guide walks through the Polish plural rules step by step: the endings for feminine, neuter and non-personal masculine nouns, the masculine-personal plural with its consonant changes, adjective and verb agreement, irregular plurals such as *ludzie* and *dzieci*, nouns that only exist in the plural, and what happens after numbers like *pięć*. There is a practice section with answers at the end.

## The Big Picture: Two Plural Genders

In the singular, every noun is masculine, feminine or neuter. If you need a refresher on how to tell them apart, our guide to [understanding Polish gender](/blog/understanding-polish-gender) covers the endings and the exceptions. In the Polish plural, those three genders collapse into two groups.

| Plural group | Polish name | Who is in it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Masculine-personal | rodzaj męskoosobowy | male people, and mixed groups that include at least one man | studenci, Polacy, panowie |
| Non-masculine-personal | rodzaj niemęskoosobowy | everything else: women, children, animals, objects, ideas | studentki, koty, książki, okna |

This is why Polish has two words for "they": **oni** for a group that includes at least one man, and **one** for every other group. A room full of women is *one*. A room full of women plus one man is *oni*. A group of cats is *one*, even if every cat is male.

The rest of this guide follows that split. First the "everything else" group, which uses a small set of endings, then the masculine-personal plural, which is where the real Polish plural rules live.

## Polish Plural Rules for Feminine and Non-Personal Masculine Nouns

Feminine nouns and masculine nouns that are not people (objects and animals) share the same three Polish plural endings in the nominative: **-y**, **-i** or **-e**. Which one you use depends on the last consonant of the stem.

| Stem ends in | Ending | Masculine (non-personal) | Feminine |
|---|---|---|---|
| most hard consonants | **-y** | kot → koty, dom → domy, stół → stoły | kobieta → kobiety, mama → mamy, lampa → lampy |
| -k, -g | **-i** | ptak → ptaki, bank → banki | książka → książki, noga → nogi |
| soft consonants and c, cz, sz, rz, ż, dż, j, l | **-e** | klucz → klucze, pokój → pokoje, hotel → hotele, koń → konie | ulica → ulice, lekcja → lekcje, ciocia → ciocie, noc → noce |

The **-i after k and g** rule is a spelling rule, not a separate pattern. Native Polish words don't spell *ky* or *gy*, so *książky* is impossible: it must be *książki*.

### Small changes inside the stem

Watch for two common changes in the stem when you form the Polish plural of these nouns:

- **A "mobile" e disappears:** *pies → psy, ołówek → ołówki, dzień → dni*. If the *e* only exists to break up consonants in the singular, it usually drops out.
- **ó changes back to o:** *stół → stoły, pokój → pokoje, samochód → samochody*. The *ó* usually belongs to the final syllable of the singular form only.

### Feminine exceptions worth knowing

Feminine nouns that end in **-ść** take **-i**: *miłość → miłości, kość → kości, wiadomość → wiadomości*. A few other feminine nouns that end in a consonant also take **-y** or **-i** instead of **-e**: *rzecz → rzeczy* (things), *mysz → myszy* (mice). *Pani* becomes *panie* (ladies), as in the classic way to address an audience: *Panie i Panowie!* (Ladies and Gentlemen!)

## Neuter Polish Plural Nouns: -a

Neuter nouns are the simplest Polish plural nouns. Almost all of them take **-a**.

| Singular | Plural | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| okno | okna | window |
| piwo | piwa | beer |
| miasto | miasta | city |
| jabłko | jabłka | apple |
| morze | morza | sea |
| pole | pola | field |
| imię | imiona | first name |
| zwierzę | zwierzęta | animal |
| muzeum | muzea | museum |

Nouns that end in **-ę** add a stem extension in the plural: *imię → imiona, zwierzę → zwierzęta*. Nouns in **-um**, such as *muzeum* and *centrum*, drop the *-um* and add *-a*: *muzea, centra*.

The one everyday neuter noun that refuses to follow the pattern is **dziecko → dzieci** (child → children). Two body parts are irregular too: **oko → oczy** (eyes) and **ucho → uszy** (ears). We will come back to them in the irregular section.

## The Masculine-Personal Polish Plural (Męskoosobowy)

This is the "aha" section of the Polish plural. Masculine nouns that refer to **male human beings** have their own Polish plural form, and in most cases it changes the final consonant of the stem. There are three main patterns.

### Pattern 1: -i / -y with a consonant change

The most common pattern softens the last consonant, then adds *-i* or *-y*. The changes are regular, so it pays to learn them as a table rather than word by word.

| Change | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| t → ci | student, poeta | studenci, poeci |
| d → dzi | sąsiad | sąsiedzi |
| st → ści | turysta, dentysta | turyści, dentyści |
| k → cy | Polak, pracownik | Polacy, pracownicy |
| g → dzy | kolega | koledzy |
| ch → si | Czech | Czesi |
| r → rzy | aktor, kelner | aktorzy, kelnerzy |
| zn → źni | mężczyzna | mężczyźni |
| -ec → -cy | chłopiec, Niemiec | chłopcy, Niemcy |

A few things to notice. *Sąsiad → sąsiedzi* also changes its vowel (*a → e*). *Niemcy* means both "Germans" and "Germany", because the country name is the plural of *Niemiec*. And the masculine-personal plural of *Polak* is *Polacy*, not *Polaki*. *Polaki* does exist, but it sounds dismissive or insulting, so avoid it.

### Pattern 2: -owie

A second group of masculine-personal Polish plural nouns takes **-owie**. It covers many titles, family roles and some professions:

| Singular | Plural | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| profesor | profesorowie | professors |
| pan | panowie | gentlemen |
| syn | synowie | sons |
| ojciec | ojcowie | fathers |
| mąż | mężowie | husbands |
| dziadek | dziadkowie | grandfathers, grandparents |
| uczeń | uczniowie | pupils |

Noun-like surnames also use *-owie* to mean "the whole family": *Nowakowie* (the Nowaks). Surnames that look like adjectives take an adjective ending instead: *Kowalscy* (the Kowalskis).

### Pattern 3: -e for soft stems

Masculine-personal nouns with a soft stem, or one ending in **-arz, -erz** or **-ciel**, take **-e** and keep their stem: *lekarz → lekarze* (doctors), *nauczyciel → nauczyciele* (teachers), *przyjaciel → przyjaciele* (friends), *gość → goście* (guests). These are still masculine-personal Polish plural nouns, which matters for the adjectives in front of them: *dobrzy lekarze*, not *dobre lekarze*.

### Fully irregular masculine-personal plurals

Three very common words have to be memorised outright: **brat → bracia** (brothers), **człowiek → ludzie** (people) and **ksiądz → księża** (priests).

## How Adjectives and Verbs Agree in the Polish Plural

The two-group split does not stop at nouns. Adjectives, pronouns and past-tense verbs all have a masculine-personal form and a form for everything else. Compare *dobrzy studenci* (good students, male or mixed) with *dobre książki* (good books) and *dobre studentki* (good female students).

### Adjectives

In the Polish plural, masculine-personal adjectives undergo the same kind of consonant softening as the nouns, then take **-i** or **-y**. Every other plural adjective simply takes **-e**.

| Singular (m) | Masculine-personal plural | Other plural |
|---|---|---|
| dobry (good) | dobrzy studenci | dobre książki |
| nowy (new) | nowi koledzy | nowe samochody |
| młody (young) | młodzi ludzie | młode kobiety |
| mały (small) | mali chłopcy | małe dzieci |
| polski (Polish) | polscy aktorzy | polskie miasta |
| wysoki (tall) | wysocy mężczyźni | wysokie drzewa |
| drogi (dear, expensive) | drodzy przyjaciele | drogie samochody |
| zły (bad, angry) | źli sąsiedzi | złe wiadomości |

*Drodzy przyjaciele!* (Dear friends!) is a common way to open a speech or a letter, and *młodzi ludzie* (young people) is a phrase you will hear constantly.

The same split runs through pronouns and determiners:

| | Masculine-personal | Other |
|---|---|---|
| they | oni | one |
| these | ci | te |
| my | moi | moje |
| our | nasi | nasze |
| all | wszyscy | wszystkie |
| which, who | którzy | które |

*Ci studenci są z Polski* (These students are from Poland) but *Te studentki są z Polski* (These female students are from Poland).

### Verbs

In the present tense, Polish plural verbs look the same for both groups: *oni są, one są; oni mają, one mają*. The difference appears in the **past tense**, where masculine-personal subjects take **-li** and everyone else takes **-ły**.

| Verb | Masculine-personal | Other |
|---|---|---|
| być (to be) | byli | były |
| mieć (to have) | mieli | miały |
| robić (to do) | robili | robiły |
| czytać (to read) | czytali | czytały |

*Studenci byli w kinie* (The students were at the cinema) but *Studentki były w kinie*. Our guide to the [Polish past tense](/blog/polish-past-tense) covers the full set of endings, including the *ł → l* switch in *byli* and *mieli*.

### The one-man rule

A group counts as masculine-personal if it contains **at least one man**. *Anna i Marek byli w Krakowie* (Anna and Marek were in Kraków) uses *byli*, because Marek is in the group. *Anna i Ola były w Krakowie* uses *były*. Children are a special case: *dzieci* is a non-masculine-personal noun, so *dzieci były* is correct even if every child in the room is a boy.

## Irregular Polish Plural Nouns

A handful of very common nouns have a Polish plural that you simply have to learn. The table below includes the genitive plural as well, because you need it after numbers and quantities.

| Singular | Nominative plural | Genitive plural | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| człowiek | ludzie | ludzi | person → people |
| rok | lata | lat | year → years |
| dziecko | dzieci | dzieci | child → children |
| oko | oczy | oczu | eye → eyes |
| ucho | uszy | uszu | ear → ears |
| ręka | ręce | rąk | hand → hands |
| brat | bracia | braci | brother → brothers |
| przyjaciel | przyjaciele | przyjaciół | friend → friends |
| tydzień | tygodnie | tygodni | week → weeks |
| ksiądz | księża | księży | priest → priests |

*Rok → lata* is the one you will need first, because it is how Poles give their age: *Mam dwadzieścia dwa lata* (I'm 22) but *Mam dwadzieścia pięć lat* (I'm 25). *Oko* and *ucho* have a regular plural too (*oka, ucha*), but only for non-body meanings such as the eyes of fat on a soup or the handles of a pot.

## Plurale Tantum: Polish Nouns With No Singular

Some Polish plural nouns have no singular form at all. Linguists call these *plurale tantum*. English has a few (scissors, trousers, glasses), and Polish has many more, including several that surprise learners.

| Noun | Meaning | Genitive (nie ma…) |
|---|---|---|
| drzwi | door | drzwi |
| spodnie | trousers | spodni |
| okulary | glasses | okularów |
| nożyczki | scissors | nożyczek |
| urodziny | birthday | urodzin |
| imieniny | name day | imienin |
| skrzypce | violin | skrzypiec |
| plecy | back (body) | pleców |
| usta | mouth | ust |
| wakacje | summer holidays | wakacji |

Because these nouns are grammatically plural, everything around them takes the Polish plural too, even when you mean one object:

- *Te drzwi są zamknięte.* (This door is closed.)
- *Gdzie są moje okulary?* (Where are my glasses?)
- *Kiedy masz urodziny?* (When is your birthday?)
- *Nie mam nożyczek.* (I don't have any scissors.)

Many Polish place names belong here as well: *Katowice, Kielce, Gliwice*, and countries such as *Włochy* (Italy), *Węgry* (Hungary) and *Niemcy* (Germany). So you say *Katowice są na Śląsku* (Katowice is in Silesia), with a plural verb.

To count these nouns, Polish uses special forms: *jedne drzwi* (one door), *dwoje drzwi* (two doors), or a measure word such as *dwie pary spodni* (two pairs of trousers).

## The Genitive Plural After Numbers: Pięć Kotów, Pięć Książek

Numbers are where the Polish plural meets the genitive case. The rule depends on the number:

| Number | Form of the noun | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | nominative singular | jeden kot, jedna książka, jedno okno |
| 2, 3, 4 (and 22–24, 32–34…) | nominative plural | dwa koty, dwie książki, trzy okna |
| 5 and up (including 12–14) | genitive plural | pięć kotów, pięć książek, pięć okien |

Note *dwa* for masculine and neuter nouns but *dwie* for feminine ones: *dwa koty, dwa okna*, but *dwie książki*. Compound numbers ending in *jeden* also take the genitive plural: *dwadzieścia jeden kotów* (21 cats).

![Five kittens sitting together on a lawn, which in Polish is pięć kotków: after five, the Polish plural switches to the genitive — Photo by Pixabay on Pexels](/blog/polish-plural-kittens.webp)

### Genitive plural endings

The genitive plural has its own set of endings, and the most surprising one is the "zero ending", where the noun simply loses its final vowel.

| Group | Ending | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Masculine, hard stem | **-ów** | kot → kotów, dom → domów, student → studentów |
| Masculine, soft stem | **-i / -y** | nauczyciel → nauczycieli, lekarz → lekarzy, pokój → pokoi |
| Feminine in -a | **zero** (vowel drops) | kobieta → kobiet, książka → książek, siostra → sióstr, ulica → ulic |
| Feminine soft (-ja, -ść, consonant) | **-i / -y** | lekcja → lekcji, miłość → miłości, noc → nocy |
| Neuter | **zero** | okno → okien, jabłko → jabłek, miasto → miast |

When the zero ending would leave a hard cluster, Polish inserts an *e* (*książek, okien, jabłek*), or turns *o* into *ó* (*siostra → sióstr, noga → nóg*).

### Masculine-personal nouns with numbers

Masculine-personal nouns follow their own number rules. With 2–4 you can say *dwaj studenci* or, very often in everyday speech, *dwóch studentów*. From 5 upward the number itself changes form: *pięciu studentów* (five students), *sześciu Polaków* (six Poles).

After 5 and higher, the verb goes into the third-person singular (neuter in the past tense): *Pięć kotów śpi na kanapie* (Five cats are sleeping on the sofa), *Było tam pięciu studentów* (There were five students there). ## Scenario: Planning a Birthday Party

Two flatmates plan a party. Every Polish plural form is in bold.

> **Ania:** Kto przychodzi na **urodziny**?
> *Who's coming to the birthday party?*
>
> **Tomek:** **Moi koledzy** z pracy, **twoje siostry** i **nasi sąsiedzi**.
> *My friends from work, your sisters and our neighbours.*
>
> **Ania:** **Sąsiedzi**? **Oni** są **mili**?
> *The neighbours? Are they nice?*
>
> **Tomek:** Tak, bardzo. Mają **dwa psy** i **troje dzieci**.
> *Yes, very. They have two dogs and three children.*
>
> **Ania:** To potrzebujemy **dziesięć krzeseł** i **dwadzieścia talerzy**.
> *Then we need ten chairs and twenty plates.*
>
> **Tomek:** I **świeczki**! Bez świeczek to nie są **urodziny**.
> *And candles! Without candles it's not a birthday.*

Notice *troje dzieci* (three children). *Dzieci* is counted with the collective numerals *dwoje, troje, czworo, pięcioro*, just like *drzwi*.

## Common Mistakes With the Polish Plural

1. **Using -y after k or g:** *książky, nogy* ✗ → *książki, nogi* ✓
2. **Forgetting the masculine-personal form:** *studenty, Polaki* ✗ → *studenci, Polacy* ✓
3. **Wrong adjective with a men's group:** *dobre studenci* ✗ → *dobrzy studenci* ✓
4. **Wrong past tense with a mixed group:** *Anna i Marek były* ✗ → *Anna i Marek byli* ✓
5. **Treating dzieci as masculine-personal:** *dzieci byli* ✗ → *dzieci były* ✓
6. **Nominative after five:** *pięć koty* ✗ → *pięć kotów* ✓
7. **Singular verb with plurale tantum:** *drzwi jest otwarte* ✗ → *drzwi są otwarte* ✓

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the basic Polish plural rules?

Feminine and non-personal masculine nouns take **-y** (most hard stems), **-i** (after k, g) or **-e** (soft stems). Neuter nouns take **-a**. Masculine nouns for men take **-i/-y** with a consonant change (*student → studenci*), **-owie** (*profesor → profesorowie*) or **-e** (*lekarz → lekarze*).

### What is the masculine-personal plural?

It is the plural used for male human beings and for mixed groups with at least one man. It has its own noun, adjective, pronoun and past-tense forms: *ci dobrzy studenci byli*, compared with *te dobre studentki były*.

### Why is it "pięć kotów" and not "pięć koty"?

After 5 and higher (and after 12–14), the Polish plural switches to the genitive. Only 2, 3 and 4 (and compounds ending in them, like 22–24) take the nominative plural: *dwa koty*, but *pięć kotów*.

### Is "drzwi" singular or plural in Polish?

*Drzwi* is always plural, even for one door. It takes plural adjectives and verbs: *Te drzwi są otwarte.* The same goes for *okulary, spodnie, nożyczki, urodziny* and *imieniny*.

### What is the Polish plural of "człowiek"?

It is *ludzie* (people), with the genitive plural *ludzi*: *Tu jest dużo ludzi* (There are a lot of people here).

### Do Polish plural nouns change in other cases too?

Yes. Every case has its own plural endings, but several are shared across genders: the dative plural is always *-om*, the instrumental plural is almost always *-ami* (with exceptions such as *dziećmi* and *ludźmi*) and the locative plural is *-ach*. Our guide to the [Polish dative case](/blog/polish-dative-case) shows the *-om* plural in action.

The Polish plural rewards learners who look at patterns instead of single words. Learn the *-y / -i / -e / -a* endings, the consonant changes of the masculine-personal plural, and the short list of irregulars, and most plurals will fall into place on their own. For a linguist's view of nouns that only exist in the plural across many languages, the [plurale tantum article on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plurale_tantum) is a good next read.
