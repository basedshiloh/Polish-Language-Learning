The Polish past tense is the first big step after the present tense, and it is friendlier than it looks. There is only one everyday past tense in Polish, it is built from the infinitive you already know, and the endings follow one pattern for almost every verb. If you can say *czytam*, *robię* and *piszę*, you are a few minutes away from *czytałem*, *robiłam* and *pisaliśmy*.

The catch is gender. In the present tense, *jestem* works for everyone. In the past, a man says *byłem* and a woman says *byłam*, and groups split into "at least one man" and "no men at all." That sounds like extra work, but it is one small table you will use in every conversation about yesterday, last weekend or your childhood.

This guide walks through the Polish past tense step by step: how to build it from the infinitive, the full person and gender table, the *-li* versus *-ły* plural, the endings that like to jump onto other words, the most common stem changes and irregular verbs, how aspect changes the meaning, and the mistakes learners make most often. Practice sentences with answers are at the end.

## How the Polish Past Tense Works

English has several past tenses: *I did*, *I was doing*, *I have done*, *I had done*. The Polish past tense covers all of them with a single set of forms. *Czytałem* can mean "I read," "I was reading" or "I have been reading," depending on context. The extra nuance English packs into tenses, Polish handles with **aspect**, which we will get to later.

What the Polish past tense does track is **person, number and gender**. Every past form tells you who did the action (I, you, he, we...), whether it was one person or several, and whether the subject was masculine, feminine or neuter. That is why you will often see past-tense tables with five columns instead of the two you know from the present.

The good news is that the three present-tense conjugation groups stop mattering here. Group I *czytać* (*czytam, czytasz*), Group II *robić* (*robię, robisz*) and Group III *pisać* (*piszę, piszesz*) all form the past in exactly the same way. If you want a refresher on those groups before you start, the site's reference page lays them side by side.

## Building the Polish Past Tense from the Infinitive

Here is the core recipe for the Polish past tense. Take the infinitive, remove the final **-ć**, and add **-ł-** plus a personal ending.

| Infinitive | Stem (drop -ć) | Past stem | "he did" |
|---|---|---|---|
| czytać (to read) | czyta- | czytał- | czytał |
| robić (to do) | robi- | robił- | robił |
| pisać (to write) | pisa- | pisał- | pisał |
| mówić (to speak) | mówi- | mówił- | mówił |
| gotować (to cook) | gotowa- | gotował- | gotował |
| być (to be) | by- | był- | był |

Notice the *-ować* verbs. In the present tense *gotować* turns into *gotuję*, but in the past tense it goes straight back to the infinitive: *gotował*, never \**gotujał*. The same is true of *pracować → pracował*, *kupować → kupował* and *studiować → studiował*. For these verbs the Polish past tense is actually easier than the present.

The **-ł-** is the heart of every past form. It is pronounced like English *w* in "water," so *był* sounds roughly like "bew" and *czytała* like "chi-TA-wa." Once you hear that *w* sound at the end of a verb, your brain can file it under "past."

## Polish Past Tense Endings: The Full Table

Now for the personal endings. These are the Polish past tense endings you add after the *-ł-*:

| Person | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
|---|---|---|---|
| ja (I) | -łem | -łam | — |
| ty (you) | -łeś | -łaś | — |
| on / ona / ono | -ł | -ła | -ło |

| Person | Masculine-personal | Non-masculine-personal |
|---|---|---|
| my (we) | -liśmy | -łyśmy |
| wy (you all) | -liście | -łyście |
| oni / one (they) | -li | -ły |

The neuter column for *ja* and *ty* is technically possible (*byłom*, *byłoś*), but you will almost never need it. It turns up mainly in fairy tales or when a child or an object "speaks." For real conversations, learn the masculine and feminine columns.

## Polish Past Tense Conjugation of Być

The most useful verb to learn first is *być*, "to be." Here is the complete Polish past tense conjugation, checked against the [Wiktionary conjugation table for *być*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/by%C4%87#Polish):

| Person | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
|---|---|---|---|
| ja | byłem | byłam | — |
| ty | byłeś | byłaś | — |
| on / ona / ono | był | była | było |

| Person | Masculine-personal | Non-masculine-personal |
|---|---|---|
| my | byliśmy | byłyśmy |
| wy | byliście | byłyście |
| oni / one | byli | były |

A few example sentences:

- **Byłem w Krakowie.** – I was in Kraków. (a man speaking)
- **Byłam w Krakowie.** – I was in Kraków. (a woman speaking)
- **Gdzie byłeś wczoraj?** – Where were you yesterday? (to a man)
- **Gdzie byłaś wczoraj?** – Where were you yesterday? (to a woman)
- **Było zimno.** – It was cold.
- **Byliśmy zmęczeni.** – We were tired. (a group with at least one man)
- **Byłyśmy zmęczone.** – We were tired. (a group of women)

That last pair shows something useful: the adjective after *być* agrees too (*zmęczeni* vs *zmęczone*). The neuter *było* is also the go-to form for weather and general impressions: *Było fajnie* ("It was great"), *Było późno* ("It was late").

If you want to compare these with the present-tense forms, the *być* reference page has the full present table and the rule about the instrumental case after *być*, which works the same in the past: *Byłem studentem*, "I was a student."

## Gender in the Polish Past Tense

In the singular, the Polish past tense rule is simple: the verb agrees with the speaker or subject. A man always says *byłem, robiłem, czytałem*. A woman always says *byłam, robiłam, czytałam*. When you speak to someone, the *ty* form matches their gender: *Byłeś?* to a man, *Byłaś?* to a woman.

Formal address uses the third person. With *pan* (sir) and *pani* (madam), the verb goes in the *on* or *ona* form: *Czy pan był już w Polsce?* ("Have you been to Poland yet, sir?") and *Czy pani była już w Polsce?* ("Have you been to Poland yet, madam?").

If noun gender is still a bit fuzzy, it is worth revisiting, because the Polish past tense uses the same masculine, feminine and neuter split. Our guide to [understanding Polish noun gender](/blog/understanding-polish-gender) covers how to tell them apart from the ending. Past-tense agreement follows the noun: *Pociąg był pełny* (the train, masculine), *Kawa była zimna* (the coffee, feminine), *Okno było otwarte* (the window, neuter).

## Masculine-Personal vs Non-Masculine-Personal: -li or -ły?

In the plural, the Polish past tense does not split by masculine, feminine and neuter. It asks one question: **is there at least one man in the group?**

- **Masculine-personal (-li):** groups of men, or mixed groups of men and women. *Oni byli*, *my byliśmy*, *wy byliście*.
- **Non-masculine-personal (-ły):** everyone and everything else, meaning groups of women, children, animals and things. *One były*, *my byłyśmy*, *wy byłyście*.

Some examples:

- **Piotr i Anna byli w kinie.** – Piotr and Anna were at the cinema. (mixed group → *-li*)
- **Anna i Kasia były w kinie.** – Anna and Kasia were at the cinema. (women only → *-ły*)
- **Dzieci były głodne.** – The children were hungry. (*dzieci* is grammatically non-masculine-personal)
- **Książki były drogie.** – The books were expensive.
- **Psy szczekały całą noc.** – The dogs barked all night. (animals → *-ły*, even male dogs)

One man is enough to switch a whole group to *-li*. If ten women and one man went to a concert, Polish says *byli na koncercie*.

### The ł→l Switch

Look closely at the masculine-personal endings: *byli*, *robili*, *czytali*, *pisaliśmy*. The **ł** has become a plain **l**. This happens because *ł* cannot stand before the vowel *i* in these endings, so it softens to *l*. Everywhere else, including all the *-ły* forms, the *ł* stays: *były*, *robiły*, *czytałyśmy*.

So the masculine-personal plural has two markers at once: the vowel *i* instead of *y*, and *l* instead of *ł*. Writing \**byłi* is one of the most common spelling slips learners make. If you remember that "*li* goes with men, *ły* goes with everyone else," the spelling takes care of itself.

## The Mobile Polish Past Tense Endings (-m, -ś, -śmy, -ście)

Here is a feature that surprises many learners. The personal endings of the Polish past tense (*-(e)m*, *-(e)ś*, *-śmy*, *-ście*) are not glued to the verb as firmly as endings in other tenses. Historically they come from old present-tense forms of *być*, and in speech they can still detach and attach to an earlier word in the sentence, usually a question word or a pronoun.

| Standard (on the verb) | Colloquial (moved forward) | English |
|---|---|---|
| Gdzie byłeś? | Gdzieś był? | Where were you? |
| Gdzie byliście? | Gdzieście byli? | Where were you (all)? |
| My już jedliśmy. | Myśmy już jedli. | We have already eaten. |
| Co ty zrobiłeś? | Coś ty zrobił? | What have you done? |

When the ending moves, the verb is left in its bare third-person form (*był*, *byli*, *jedli*, *zrobił*). That is why *Gdzieś był?* means "Where were *you*?" and not "Where was he?" The *-ś* on *gdzie* carries the "you."

**Treat these as colloquial.** You will hear *Gdzieś był?* and *Coś ty zrobił?* from native speakers, in films and in older literature, so you should recognise them. In your own speaking and writing, keep the ending on the verb: *Gdzie byłeś?* is always correct and never sounds odd. Be careful with *coś*, too, since it is also the ordinary word for "something."

### A Quick Note on Stress

Polish normally stresses the second-to-last syllable. The *-śmy* and *-ście* forms are a known exception: prescriptive grammars put the stress one syllable earlier, so careful speech says *BY-li-śmy* and *zro-BI-li-śmy*. As the overview of [Polish phonology on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_phonology) notes, this rule is often ignored in everyday speech, and many people stress *zro-bi-LI-śmy*. Either way you will be understood.

## Stem Changes: -eć Verbs and the e→a Alternation

Verbs whose infinitive ends in **-eć** add one twist to the Polish past tense. In most past forms the **e** changes to **a** before *ł*, but in the masculine-personal plural the **e** comes back.

| Infinitive | Masculine sg. | Feminine sg. | Masc.-personal pl. | Non-masc.-personal pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| mieć (to have) | miał | miała | mieli | miały |
| chcieć (to want) | chciał | chciała | chcieli | chciały |
| widzieć (to see) | widział | widziała | widzieli | widziały |
| wiedzieć (to know) | wiedział | wiedziała | wiedzieli | wiedziały |
| rozumieć (to understand) | rozumiał | rozumiała | rozumieli | rozumiały |
| umieć (can, know how) | umiał | umiała | umieli | umiały |
| musieć (must) | musiał | musiała | musieli | musiały |

The full Polish past tense conjugation of *mieć* shows the pattern clearly:

| Person | Masculine | Feminine |
|---|---|---|
| ja | miałem | miałam |
| ty | miałeś | miałaś |
| on / ona / ono | miał | miała (ono: miało) |
| my | mieliśmy | miałyśmy |
| wy | mieliście | miałyście |
| oni / one | mieli | miały |

A handy way to remember it: **a** before **ł**, **e** before **li**. So *miałem, miała, miały*, but *mieli, mieliśmy, mieliście*. Learners often write \**miałi* or \**miełem*. Neither exists.

Three of the verbs in that table, *wiedzieć*, *umieć* and *rozumieć*, are worth practising together, because they are among the first verbs you need when you talk about the past: *Nie wiedziałem* ("I didn't know"), *Nie rozumiałam* ("I didn't understand"), *Umieli pływać* ("They knew how to swim").

## Irregular Verbs in the Polish Past Tense

Most verbs follow the "drop -ć, add -ł-" recipe for the Polish past tense. A small group of very common verbs do not, because their infinitive ends in *-ść*, *-c* or *-ąć*. These you simply learn.

### Iść (to go on foot)

| Person | Masculine | Feminine |
|---|---|---|
| ja | szedłem | szłam |
| ty | szedłeś | szłaś |
| on / ona / ono | szedł | szła (ono: szło) |
| my | szliśmy | szłyśmy |
| wy | szliście | szłyście |
| oni / one | szli | szły |

The masculine singular keeps an **e** (*szedł, szedłem*), while every other form drops it (*szła, szłam, szli*). The same pattern runs through all the prefixed forms: *pójść → poszedł, poszła, poszli*; *przyjść → przyszedł, przyszła, przyszli*.

### Jeść (to eat)

*Jeść* gives *jadł, jadła, jadło, jadły* with **a**, but switches back to **e** in the masculine-personal plural: *jedli, jedliśmy, jedliście*. A man says *jadłem*, a woman *jadłam*, and a mixed group *jedliśmy*. The perfective *zjeść* works the same way: *zjadłem, zjadła, zjedli*.

### Móc (to be able, can)

*Móc* has an **ó** only in the bare masculine form *mógł*. Everywhere else it is **o**: *mogłem, mogłam, mogła, mogło, mogli, mogły, mogliśmy*. So: *Nie mógł przyjść* ("He couldn't come"), but *Nie mogłam przyjść* ("I couldn't come," a woman speaking).

### Verbs in -ąć

Verbs like *wziąć* (to take) and *zacząć* (to start) keep **ą** only in the masculine singular forms and switch to **ę** elsewhere:

| | Masculine sg. | Feminine sg. | Masc.-personal pl. |
|---|---|---|---|
| wziąć | wziąłem, wziął | wzięłam, wzięła | wzięli |
| zacząć | zacząłem, zaczął | zaczęłam, zaczęła | zaczęli |

### Być Is (Almost) Regular

It is worth stressing that *być*, irregular in the present (*jestem, jesteś, jest*), is perfectly regular in the Polish past tense. *By-* + *-ł-* + ending gives *byłem, byłaś, byli* and the rest. Its future (*będę*) is a different story, which we cover in a separate guide.

## Aspect in the Past: Robiłem vs Zrobiłem

Most Polish verbs come in pairs: an **imperfective** verb for an ongoing, repeated or unfinished action, and a **perfective** verb for a single completed one. In the present tense you mostly meet the imperfective member. In the Polish past tense you need both, because the choice changes the meaning.

| Imperfective | Perfective | Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Robiłem zadanie. | Zrobiłem zadanie. | I was doing / worked on the homework. vs I did (finished) the homework. |
| Czytałam książkę. | Przeczytałam książkę. | I was reading / read some of the book. vs I read the book (to the end). |
| Pisał list. | Napisał list. | He was writing a letter. vs He wrote (finished) a letter. |
| Jedliśmy obiad. | Zjedliśmy obiad. | We were having lunch. vs We ate (finished) lunch. |
| Kupowała chleb codziennie. | Kupiła chleb. | She bought bread every day. vs She bought (some) bread. |

A quick rule of thumb: if English could say "was doing," "used to do" or "did regularly," reach for the imperfective. If English means "got it done," "finished" or "managed to," use the perfective. That is why *Wczoraj czytałam, a potem oglądałam telewizję* ("Yesterday I was reading and then watching TV") describes how you spent the evening, while *Przeczytałam całą książkę* ("I read the whole book") reports a result.

Aspect is a big topic on its own, and the good news is that the past forms of perfective verbs are built in exactly the same way: *zrobić → zrobiłem*, *przeczytać → przeczytałam*. For the full explanation of pairs and how to recognise them, see our guide to [Polish verb aspect](/blog/polish-verb-aspect).

## Scenario: Talking About the Weekend

Here is a short, realistic exchange between two friends on a Monday morning, packed with the Polish past tense. Notice how the gender endings change with each speaker.

> **Marek:** Cześć, Ola! Co robiłaś w weekend?
> *Hi, Ola! What did you do at the weekend?*
>
> **Ola:** Byłam u rodziców w Gdańsku. A ty? Gdzie byłeś?
> *I was at my parents' in Gdańsk. And you? Where were you?*
>
> **Marek:** Nigdzie. Byłem w domu i oglądałem filmy.
> *Nowhere. I was at home and watched films.*
>
> **Ola:** A Kasia i Tomek?
> *And Kasia and Tomek?*
>
> **Marek:** Byli w górach. Kasia mówiła, że było zimno, ale pięknie.
> *They were in the mountains. Kasia said it was cold but beautiful.*
>
> **Ola:** Zazdroszczę! My z mamą tylko gotowałyśmy i jadłyśmy.
> *I'm jealous! My mum and I just cooked and ate.*

Every Polish past tense form here follows the rules above: *robiłaś* and *byłam* (woman), *byłeś*, *byłem*, *oglądałem* (man), *byli* (mixed group), *było* (impersonal), and *gotowałyśmy*, *jadłyśmy* (two women).

## Common Mistakes with the Polish Past Tense

These are the Polish past tense errors that come up again and again with learners. Each one is easy to fix once you have seen it.

### 1. Using the wrong gender for yourself

A woman saying \**Byłem zmęczony* is the classic slip, often copied from a textbook written from a male point of view. Pick your forms once and drill them: *byłam, robiłam, chciałam* or *byłem, robiłem, chciałem*.

### 2. Using -li for a group of women

*Moje siostry były w Warszawie*, not \**byli*. Only groups with at least one man take *-li*.

### 3. Forgetting the ł→l switch

\**Byłi*, \**robiłi* and \**czytałiśmy* do not exist. The masculine-personal plural always has *l*: *byli, robili, czytaliśmy*.

### 4. Keeping e or a in the wrong place

With *-eć* verbs, it is *a* before *ł* and *e* before *li*: *miałem* but *mieli*, *chciała* but *chcieli*. Avoid \**miełem* and \**chciali*.

### 5. Regularising iść, jeść and móc

\**Iśłem*, \**jedłem* and \**mógłem* are understandable attempts but wrong. The correct forms are *szedłem*, *jadłem* and *mogłem*.

### 6. Building -ować verbs from the present

\**Pracujałem* is a frequent error. The Polish past tense of *pracować* is built from the infinitive: *pracowałem*.

### 7. Adding an extra "być"

English-speakers sometimes try \**Ja byłem robiłem*, translating "I was doing." Polish needs only one word: *robiłem*.

## Practice: Polish Past Tense Exercises

Put the verb in brackets into the correct Polish past tense form. The speaker or subject is given so you can choose the right gender. Answers are below.

1. (Ania) Wczoraj ____ w kinie. *(być)*
2. (Paweł) ____ list do babci. *(napisać)*
3. (my: Ola and Kasia) ____ pizzę. *(jeść)*
4. (oni: Marek and Ewa) ____ w Krakowie. *(mieszkać)*
5. (ty, to a man) Gdzie ____ w sobotę? *(być)*
6. (ja, a woman) Nie ____ przyjść. *(móc)*
7. (wy: Tomek and Piotr) ____ ten film? *(widzieć)*
8. (on) ____ do pracy pieszo. *(iść)*
9. (dzieci) ____ bardzo głodne. *(być)*
10. (ja, a man) ____ kawę, ale nie było mleka. *(chcieć)*

### Answers

1. **Wczoraj była w kinie.** – Yesterday she was at the cinema.
2. **Napisał list do babci.** – He wrote a letter to his grandma.
3. **Jadłyśmy pizzę.** – We were eating pizza.
4. **Mieszkali w Krakowie.** – They lived in Kraków.
5. **Gdzie byłeś w sobotę?** – Where were you on Saturday?
6. **Nie mogłam przyjść.** – I couldn't come.
7. **Widzieliście ten film?** – Have you seen this film?
8. **Szedł do pracy pieszo.** – He was walking to work.
9. **Były bardzo głodne.** – They were very hungry.
10. **Chciałem kawę, ale nie było mleka.** – I wanted coffee, but there was no milk.

Sentence 10 contains a bonus pattern: *nie było* + genitive (*mleka*) is how Polish says "there was no..." in the past. It is the past version of *nie ma* + genitive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many past tenses does Polish have?

In modern everyday Polish there is just one Polish past tense. It covers English "did," "was doing," "has done" and "had done." Differences in meaning come from aspect (*robiłem* vs *zrobiłem*) and from context, not from separate tenses. An old pluperfect form exists in literature, but you do not need it to speak or understand everyday Polish.

### What are the Polish past tense endings?

After the past stem in *-ł-*, the singular endings are *-em* (masculine I), *-am* (feminine I), *-eś* (masculine you), *-aś* (feminine you), and nothing, *-a* or *-o* for he, she and it. In the plural, the Polish past tense endings are *-liśmy / -łyśmy* (we), *-liście / -łyście* (you all) and *-li / -ły* (they).

### Why does "byli" have an l but "były" has ł?

Before the *i* of the masculine-personal endings, *ł* changes to *l*. So the masculine-personal forms all show *l* (*byli, robili, mieli*), while the non-masculine-personal forms keep *ł* (*były, robiły, miały*).

### Do I need to learn the neuter "byłom" and "byłoś"?

Not for everyday use. Third-person neuter forms like *było* are very common, but first- and second-person neuter forms are rare and appear mostly in stories where a child or an object is speaking.

### Is "Gdzieś był?" correct Polish?

Yes, but it is colloquial. The *-ś* ending has moved from the verb to *gdzie*. It means the same as *Gdzie byłeś?*, which is the form learners should use in their own speech and writing.

### How do I know whether to use robiłem or zrobiłem?

Use the imperfective (*robiłem*) for actions in progress, repeated actions or when the result does not matter. Use the perfective (*zrobiłem*) for a single, finished action with a result. If you would add "finished" or "managed to" in English, choose the perfective.

Once these forms feel comfortable, try a five-minute habit: every evening, write three sentences about your day in the Polish past tense. *Rano piłam kawę. Pracowałam do piątej. Wieczorem czytałam książkę.* Three lines a day add up quickly, and within a couple of weeks *byłem* or *byłam* will come out without thinking.
