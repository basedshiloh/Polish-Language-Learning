Polish superstitions are less about fear and more about habit: the thumb squeeze before a friend's job interview, the knock on a wooden table after saying something hopeful, the hand quickly pulled back when someone offers a handshake across a doorway. Most Poles will tell you they don't really believe any of it. Then they do it anyway.

That gap between belief and habit is what makes Polish superstitions fun to learn. They come with their own phrases, many of which turn up in everyday speech even among people who would never call themselves superstitious. Knowing *trzymam kciuki* or *odpukać w niemalowane* will help you follow real conversations, not just folklore books.

This guide groups the most common Polish superstitions by occasion: everyday life at home, Christmas Eve, weddings, exams, New Year and new babies. For each one you get the Polish phrase, an English translation and, where a source actually explains it, a note on where the belief comes from.

## Przesądy: How Superstitious Are Poles, Really?

The Polish word for superstitions is **przesądy** (psheh-SON-dih; singular *przesąd*). You will also hear **zabobony**, which means much the same thing but often sounds more dismissive, closer to "old wives' tales." Someone who takes them seriously is **przesądny** (or **przesądna** for a woman).

Unusually, we have hard numbers on Polish superstitions. In 2018 the national polling centre CBOS asked a representative sample of 999 adults about ten common beliefs and habits. The report, [*Przesądny jak Polak*](https://cbos.pl/SPISKOM.POL/2018/K_093_18.PDF) ("Superstitious like a Pole"), found that:

- **54%** of adult Poles said they believed in at least one of the ten superstitions.
- Only **17%** said they never followed any of the ten superstitious habits, even occasionally.
- The most popular belief was **keeping your thumbs held** for luck: 33% believed it works, and 75% said they do it at least now and then.
- **53%** knock on unpainted wood "so as not to jinx it."

So belief is the minority position, but the habits are mainstream. That is the key to understanding Polish superstitions: they are part of the social language, a bit like saying "bless you" after a sneeze.

## Everyday Polish Superstitions at Home and on the Street

These are the Polish superstitions you are most likely to meet in daily life, in a flat in Warsaw or a kitchen in Chicago.

### Trzymać kciuki: Thumbs, Not Fingers

English speakers cross their fingers. Poles **trzymają kciuki** (literally "hold thumbs"): you tuck your thumb inside your fist and squeeze. The phrase is used constantly, even without the gesture.

- *Trzymam kciuki!* — I'm keeping my fingers crossed for you!
- *Trzymaj kciuki za mnie, jutro mam egzamin.* — Keep your fingers crossed for me, I have an exam tomorrow.

According to the CBOS survey, this is the single most practised of all Polish superstitions, so learn this one first.

### Odpukać w niemalowane drewno

When Poles say something hopeful ("the car has never broken down"), they knock on wood. The full version is **odpukać w niemalowane drewno**, "to knock on unpainted wood," and purists really do look for bare wood rather than a painted table. In conversation it usually shrinks to a quick *odpukać!* or *odpukuję w niemalowane*, often with a real knock on the nearest surface.

The reason, as the CBOS question puts it, is *żeby nie zapeszyć*: so as not to jinx it. It came second only to thumb-holding among the Polish superstitions in the survey.

### Nie przez próg! Never Greet Across a Threshold

One of the most noticeable Polish superstitions for visitors: don't shake hands or hug across a doorway. If you try, you will probably hear **Nie przez próg!** ("Not across the threshold!") and the other person will step inside or out to meet you.

In the CBOS survey, 36% of adults said they make a point of not greeting across a threshold, and 20% believed doing so brings bad luck. A Polish learning resource explains the folk logic: the **próg** (threshold) was seen as the boundary between the safe home and the dangerous world outside, so greeting across it risked letting bad forces in. Stepping onto the threshold, or fully through it, solves the problem.

### Sitting Down When You Go Back for Something

Forgot your keys and had to run back home? Many Poles will **przysiąść na chwilę**, sit down for a moment, before setting off again, to cancel out the bad luck of turning back. Some count to ten while they do it. CBOS found that 28% of adults do this at least occasionally, which makes it one of the quieter Polish superstitions you might never notice.

### Spilled Salt, Broken Mirrors and Black Cats

Some Polish superstitions are shared with much of Europe, but each has its own Polish phrase. **Rozsypana sól** (spilled salt) means a quarrel is coming in the family. A common explanation is that salt was once expensive, so spilling it felt like a real misfortune, blamed on evil spirits who wanted the household to fight. The fix: throw a pinch of the spilled salt over your left shoulder.

**Stłuczone lustro** (a broken mirror) promises *siedem lat nieszczęścia*, seven years of bad luck. In the CBOS data it is the most widely believed bad-luck omen among Polish superstitions, at 26%.

**Czarny kot** (a black cat) crossing your path is the familiar one. If you hear *Czarny kot przebiegł mi drogę*, someone is announcing that their day is doomed. About 15% of Poles say they sometimes turn back when it happens.

### The Chimney Sweep and Your Button

This one surprises most foreigners. Meeting a **kominiarz** (chimney sweep) counts as good luck, and the traditional move is to grab one of your own buttons (*złap się za guzik!*) the moment you see him. In the CBOS survey, 39% of adults said they grab a button at the sight of a chimney sweep at least now and then.

### More Small Rituals

A few smaller Polish superstitions round out the everyday list:

- **Wstać lewą nogą** — to get out of bed with your left foot. It is also a normal idiom: *Wstałeś lewą nogą?* means "Did you get up on the wrong side of the bed?"
- **Piątek trzynastego** — Friday the 13th. One in five Poles said they are extra careful that day.
- **Chuchać na monetę** — blowing on a coin you find, *na szczęście*. Over half of respondents said they do it.
- **Gwizdanie w domu** — whistling indoors. Many Polish families frown on it, though the explanations vary from bad luck to inviting evil spirits, and there is no single agreed story.

## Polish Christmas Superstitions on Wigilia

If Polish superstitions have a peak season, it is Christmas Eve. The saying **Jaka Wigilia, taki cały rok** ("As Christmas Eve goes, so goes the whole year") explains why: everything that happens on 24 December is supposed to set the tone for the next twelve months. Our full guide to [Wigilia, the Polish Christmas Eve supper](/blog/wigilia-polish-christmas-eve) covers the meal itself; here are the Polish Christmas superstitions that cluster around it.

**A carp scale in your wallet.** Carp is the signature Wigilia fish, and many Poles dry one of its scales and keep it in their wallet (*łuska karpia w portfelu*) so money "sticks" to them all year. Don't take it out until the next Christmas.

**The first guest should be a man.** One traditional belief says the first person to cross your threshold on Wigilia should be a man; a woman arriving first was thought to bring bad luck.

**Don't argue, cry or lend money.** Following *Jaka Wigilia, taki cały rok*, a quarrel or tears on Christmas Eve would mean a year of them. Lending money is also avoided, so you don't spend the year short of cash.

**Taste every dish.** Trying a little of every dish on the table is said to ensure you won't lack anything in the coming year. Some families also make sure an even number of people sit down to eat.

**Hay under the tablecloth.** A little **sianko** (hay) goes under the white tablecloth in memory of the manger. In some homes, each person pulls out a stalk after dinner, and a longer one predicts better luck for the year ahead.

**An extra place at the table.** The empty place setting is kept for an unexpected guest or in memory of absent family. It is more custom than superstition, but it belongs to the same mix of hospitality and folk belief.

### Do Animals Really Talk at Midnight?

The best-known of all Polish Christmas superstitions says that at midnight on Wigilia, animals speak with a human voice: *zwierzęta mówią ludzkim głosem*. The belief is usually linked to the animals in the stable at Bethlehem, and farm animals were traditionally given leftover food and pieces of *opłatek* after supper as thanks.

There is a catch, though. Folk tradition warns that you must never eavesdrop on them, because you might hear when your own death will come. One cautionary legend describes a cruel farmhand who listened in on his horses and heard them planning to haul the wood for his coffin.

## Polish Superstitions About Weddings: Months With an "R"

Polish wedding superstitions start with the calendar. Traditionally, a wedding should take place in a month whose Polish name contains the letter **r**:

| Month | Polish | Has an "r"? |
|---|---|---|
| March | marzec | yes |
| June | czerwiec | yes |
| August | sierpień | yes |
| September | wrzesień | yes |
| October | październik | yes |
| December | grudzień | yes |
| May | maj | no |
| July | lipiec | no |

According to *Rzeczpospolita*, the belief was once taken very seriously, and one explanation given was that it would help the couple's child pronounce the letter *r* without trouble. Modern couples clearly don't worry much: the same newspaper reports that July and August are now the most popular wedding months, and July has no *r* at all.

A Polish florist's guide to bouquet superstitions adds a few more: some Poles avoid drying the bridal bouquet afterwards, since that might "dry out" the marriage, and avoid pearls in the bouquet because they are said to bring tears. Guests sometimes reassure a couple that rain on the wedding day brings luck, a belief shared by many cultures, so it is hard to call it specifically Polish.

Before any wedding, of course, comes the question of who you will marry. For the fortune-telling side of Polish superstitions, including wax pouring and racing shoes to the door, see our guide to [Andrzejki, Poland's night of fortune-telling](/blog/andrzejki-polish-tradition).

## Polish Superstitions for Exams: From Studniówka to the Matura

Polish students have built an entire ritual system around the **matura**, the final secondary-school exam. It begins at the [studniówka, the formal ball held 100 days before the matura](/blog/studniowka-polish-prom-tradition), where many girls famously wear red underwear for luck.

From there, the most popular Polish superstitions for exams include:

- **No haircut from the studniówka until the matura.** Cut your hair and you might cut away what you have learned.
- **Wear the same red underwear** to the exam that you wore to the ball.
- **Borrow something**, usually a pen with black ink, from someone who has already passed their exams, so some of their knowledge and luck rubs off.
- **Sleep with the textbook under your pillow** the night before.
- **Get out of bed with your right foot** and step into the school with the right foot first.
- **Get a good kick** (*kopniak*) for luck just before you walk into the exam room.

### Połamania! Why Poles Don't Say "Good Luck"

Wishing someone *powodzenia* (good luck) before an exam is considered risky, so students say **Połamania pióra!** ("Break your pen!") or **Połamania nóg!** ("Break your legs!") instead, much like the theatre's "break a leg." And you shouldn't answer with *dziękuję*: thanking someone for luck wishes is said to jinx the result.

## New Year Polish Superstitions

**Sylwester** (New Year's Eve) and **Nowy Rok** (New Year's Day) have their own list of Polish superstitions, many of them about money:

- **Keep cash on you** and don't lend money on 31 December, or the year will be tight.
- **Pay your debts** before the year ends, so you don't carry them into the new one.
- **Don't clean the house or take out the rubbish** on New Year's Eve, or you might sweep your luck out with it.
- **Keep the fridge full** on 1 January; an empty fridge is said to foreshadow a poor year.
- **Wear red underwear**, ideally a pair received as a gift, for luck and love.
- **Make noise at midnight.** Fireworks echo an older Slavic belief that loud noise scares off evil forces.

Some people also hold off on laundry between Christmas and Epiphany (6 January), so that spirits don't get tangled in the washing. And many keep that Wigilia carp scale in their wallet straight through the New Year.

## Polish Superstitions About Babies: The Red Ribbon Against Urok

Walk past a few prams in any Polish town and you will likely spot a **czerwona wstążka** or **czerwona kokardka**, a red ribbon or bow, tied to the handle or hood. Its job is to protect the baby from **urok**, the evil eye. (In everyday Polish *urok* also means "charm," but *rzucić urok* means "to cast a spell.")

Adrian Mianecki, a lecturer in Płock who writes about Polish folk beliefs that survive today, gives two traditional explanations. First, the knot itself works as a closed boundary that evil can't cross. Second, red was linked in folk belief to the world of demons and the dead, so a red ribbon "disguised" the child as one of their own and a witch would leave it alone. He notes that red ribbons were also tied to cribs, door handles and even animals.

These ideas come from the same world of village belief as the creatures in our guide to [Polish folklore creatures](/blog/polish-folklore-creatures), from the noon demon in the fields to the *zmora* that sits on sleepers' chests. In most families today, the red ribbon is tied less out of fear than out of love, and very often by the baby's [babcia, the Polish grandma](/blog/grandma-in-polish) who remembers her own mother doing the same.

Pregnancy carries its own Polish superstitions too, but one study suggests they are usually taken lightly: a 2019 Polish study of 166 expectant mothers found the overall level of pregnancy superstition to be low.

## Scenario: A Black Cat on Exam Day

Ola and Kuba are walking to their matura. A black cat runs across the pavement in front of them.

- **Kuba:** *O nie! Czarny kot przebiegł nam drogę!* — Oh no! A black cat just crossed our path!
- **Ola:** *Spokojnie, ja nie jestem przesądna.* — Relax, I'm not superstitious.
- **Kuba:** *Ale masz na sobie czerwoną bieliznę ze studniówki?* — But are you wearing your red underwear from the studniówka?
- **Ola:** *No... mam. Tak na wszelki wypadek.* — Well... I am. Just in case.
- **Kuba:** *Widzisz? Dobra, trzymam kciuki. Połamania pióra!* — See? Okay, fingers crossed. Break a pen!
- **Ola:** *Nie dziękuję! Tobie też połamania!* — I'm not saying thank you! Break a pen to you too!

That last line is the perfect summary of Polish superstitions: "I don't believe in this," followed immediately by following every rule.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the most common Polish superstitions?

The most common Polish superstitions in everyday life are holding your thumbs for luck (*trzymać kciuki*), knocking on unpainted wood, not greeting across a threshold, grabbing a button when you see a chimney sweep, and fearing spilled salt, broken mirrors and black cats. A 2018 CBOS survey found that 75% of Poles hold their thumbs for someone at least occasionally.

### What does "przesądy" mean?

*Przesądy* is the Polish word for superstitions. One superstition is *przesąd*, and a superstitious person is *przesądny* (man) or *przesądna* (woman).

### What are the main Polish Christmas superstitions?

The best-known Polish Christmas superstitions are keeping a carp scale in your wallet, hoping a man is the first guest on Wigilia, avoiding arguments and lending money on Christmas Eve, tasting every dish, pulling a straw from under the tablecloth, and the belief that animals speak with a human voice at midnight.

### Why do Poles say "Nie przez próg"?

It means "Not across the threshold." Shaking hands or hugging across a doorway is believed to bring bad luck, because the threshold was traditionally seen as a boundary between the safe home and the outside world.

### Why shouldn't you say "thank you" to "Połamania"?

Among Polish students, thanking someone for wishing you luck is believed to jinx it. The joke response is to refuse the thanks or answer with a wish of your own.

### Are Polish superstitions religious or pagan?

Usually a mix of both. Many Polish superstitions, like the hay under the Wigilia tablecloth, combine Christian symbolism with older folk belief, while others, like the red ribbon on a pram, come straight from village ideas about demons and the evil eye.

### Do Poles still follow these superstitions today?

Many do, half-jokingly. CBOS found that belief in superstitions changed little between 2011 and 2018, and habits like holding thumbs and knocking on wood remain widespread even among people who say they don't believe in them.

Most Polish superstitions survive the same way family recipes do: someone's grandmother did it, so you do it too, with a slightly embarrassed smile. Learn the phrases, and the next time a Polish friend says *trzymam kciuki*, you will know exactly what to do with your thumbs.
