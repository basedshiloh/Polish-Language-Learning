The standard word for grandma in Polish is **babcia** (pronounced BAHP-chah), and it is the word you will hear in almost every Polish home, from Kraków to Chicago. But if you grew up in a Polish-American family, there is a good chance your grandmother answered to something else: Busia, Busha, Baba, or even Babci. Those words are real family words with real history, even if a dictionary in Warsaw won't list them.

This guide sorts it all out. You will learn how to say grandma in Polish the way people in Poland say it today, how the word changes in a sentence (*do babci*, *z babcią*, *babciu!*), the warmer pet forms like *babunia* and *babusia*, and the matching words for grandpa. Then we look at the diaspora side: where *busia* and *busha* most likely came from, why *jaju* is how some families spell *dziadziu*, and why *babushka* is Russian, not Polish.

If you only came for the short answer, here it is: grandma in Polish is *babcia*, grandpa is *dziadek*, and both have a whole family of affectionate variants. If you want to use them correctly on a card, a phone call or a visit, keep reading.

## Babcia: The Standard Way to Say Grandma in Polish

*Babcia* is the everyday, neutral-but-warm word for grandmother in Poland. Children use it, adults use it, and it works whether you are talking *to* your grandmother or *about* her. Wiktionary traces it to the old Slavic word *baba* plus the affectionate suffix *-cia*, which is why it already sounds a little tender to Polish ears.

**Pronunciation tip:** the first thing to get right about grandma in Polish is the sound. The **b** before **c** loses its voice, so *babcia* sounds like BAHP-chah, not BAB-see-ah. The *ci* is a soft "ch" sound, close to the "ch" in "cheese" but made with the tongue flatter against the palate. Stress, as almost always in Polish, falls on the second-to-last syllable: **BAB**-cia.

There is one word for grandma in Polish, whichever side of the family she is on. Your mother's mother and your father's mother are each *babcia*. If you need to say which one you mean, just add the side of the family:

- *babcia ze strony mamy* — grandma on mom's side
- *babcia ze strony taty* — grandma on dad's side

In real families, people usually tell the two apart by name or place instead: *babcia Zosia*, *babcia z Rzeszowa* (grandma from Rzeszów). It is exactly how English speakers say "Grandma Rose" or "the Florida grandma."

Our broader guide to [family members in Polish](/blog/family-members-in-polish) puts *babcia* in context with parents, aunts, uncles and in-laws, so it is a good companion if you are learning the whole family tree at once.

### What About Babka?

You may also see *babka*. Polish dictionaries do list it as "grandmother," and you will meet it in older texts and some regional speech. In everyday modern Polish, though, *babcia* is what people actually say, and *babka* is just as likely to mean a cake, like the famous Easter babka. If your goal is the most natural word for grandma in Polish, choose *babcia*.

## How Grandma in Polish Changes in a Sentence

Polish nouns change their endings depending on their job in the sentence, and *babcia* is no exception. This is the part that confuses heritage speakers most, because you may have heard *babci* and *babciu* at home and assumed they were separate words for grandma in Polish. They are the same word in different cases.

| Case | Form | Example | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative (subject) | **babcia** | *Babcia piecze ciasto.* | Grandma is baking a cake. |
| Genitive | **babci** | *Jadę do babci.* | I'm going to grandma's. |
| Dative | **babci** | *Daję babci kwiaty.* | I'm giving grandma flowers. |
| Accusative | **babcię** | *Kocham babcię.* | I love grandma. |
| Instrumental | **babcią** | *Mieszkam z babcią.* | I live with grandma. |
| Locative | **babci** | *Myślę o babci.* | I'm thinking about grandma. |
| Vocative (calling her) | **babciu** | *Babciu, chodź tutaj!* | Grandma, come here! |

Three forms are worth memorizing first, because they cover most family conversations:

- **do babci / u babci** — to grandma's / at grandma's house (*Jestem u babci* = I'm at grandma's)
- **z babcią** — with grandma
- **babciu** — what you say when you speak *to* her

That last one, the vocative, matters most. When you call out to your grandmother, or start a card with "Dear Grandma," you say *Babciu* or *Kochana Babciu*, never *Babcia*. Using the vocative is one of the small things that instantly makes your Polish sound like it came from a real family kitchen rather than a textbook.

## Babunia, Babusia and Other Affectionate Forms

Polish loves diminutives, and the word for grandma in Polish is a prime example. Each extra suffix adds warmth, and families tend to settle on one favorite form that becomes the grandmother's de facto name.

| Form | Pronunciation | Feel | Calling her (vocative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **babcia** | BAHP-chah | everyday, warm | *babciu* |
| **babunia** | bah-BOO-nyah | tender, a little old-fashioned | *babuniu* |
| **babusia** | bah-BOO-shah | very affectionate, childlike | *babusiu* |
| **babuleńka** | bah-boo-LEN-kah | extra tender, rather old-fashioned | *babuleńko* |

There is no strict rule for when to use which. A toddler might say *babusia*; a grown grandson might still say *babunia* on the phone because that is what he always said. The forms are not ranked by age so much as by habit and feeling.

Diminutives also work for the grandmother's own name, so you will hear combinations like *babcia Hania* (Hanna) or *babunia Basia* (Barbara). If you want to understand how these suffixes work across the whole language, our guide to [Polish diminutives](/blog/polish-diminutives) breaks down *-unia*, *-usia*, *-eńka* and the rest, with names and everyday nouns.

## Grandpa in Polish: Dziadek, Dziadzio and Dziadziuś

Once you know grandma in Polish, grandpa follows the same pattern. The partner word to *babcia* is **dziadek** (JAH-dek), grandfather. It comes from the older word *dziad* plus the suffix *-ek*. Like *babcia*, it covers both sides of the family.

| Form | Pronunciation | Feel | Calling him (vocative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **dziadek** | JAH-dek | everyday | *dziadku* |
| **dziadzio** | JAH-jo | affectionate | *dziadziu* |
| **dziadziuś** | JAH-joosh | very affectionate | *dziadziusiu* |

Notice the vocative of *dziadzio*: **dziadziu** (JAH-joo). That is the form many Polish-American families kept as grandpa's name, because it is the word children shouted across the yard. When spelled by English speakers it often turns into "Jaju," "Jadju" or "Dziadzia," which we will come back to below.

Some useful grandpa sentences:

- *Idę do dziadka.* — I'm going to grandpa's.
- *Gram w szachy z dziadkiem.* — I'm playing chess with grandpa.
- *Dziadku, pomożesz mi?* — Grandpa, will you help me?

Together, grandma and grandpa are **dziadkowie** (jahd-KOH-vyeh), the standard Polish word for "grandparents." *Jadę do dziadków na weekend* means "I'm going to my grandparents' for the weekend."

## Polish Words for Grandma and Grandpa: A Quick Reference

Here are the core Polish words for grandma and grandpa, plus the generations on either side, in one place. Start with grandma in Polish, *babcia*, and work outward. This is the table to screenshot before a family reunion.

| English | Polish | Pronunciation |
|---|---|---|
| grandma | babcia | BAHP-chah |
| grandpa | dziadek | JAH-dek |
| grandparents | dziadkowie | jahd-KOH-vyeh |
| great-grandmother | prababcia | prah-BAHP-chah |
| great-grandfather | pradziadek | prah-JAH-dek |
| great-grandparents | pradziadkowie | prah-jahd-KOH-vyeh |

The prefix **pra-** works like English "great-": add it once for one generation back, twice for two. That makes the system easy to extend when you start researching family history and find a *prapradziadek* in a parish record.

## Busia, Busha and Other Polish-American Words for Grandma

Now for the question that brings a lot of people to this page: if grandma in Polish is *babcia*, why did your family call her **Busia**?

Across the Polish diaspora in the United States, words like *Busia*, *Busha*, *Bushia*, *Baba* and *Babci* are common family names for grandmothers. A Polish-language learning blog notes that many Polish-American families use forms like *busha*, *busia* and *babusza*, and that these are not standard Polish. In Poland, a child would almost always say *babcia* or one of its diminutives.

That does not make the American words "wrong." They are a heritage version of grandma in Polish, family vocabulary shaped by a century or more of Polish spoken far from Poland, and they mean just as much to the people who use them.

### Where Does Busia Come From?

No dictionary we could find documents the origin of *busia*, so treat this as the most plausible explanation, not settled fact. The leading idea is that it is a clipped, baby-talk form of **babusia**, a genuine Polish diminutive of *babcia*. Small children often drop the first syllable of long words, and *ba-BU-sia* becomes *BU-sia* very naturally.

The pronunciation supports this. In Polish spelling, *si* sounds like a soft "sh," so *busia* is read BOO-shah. That is exactly what the American spelling **busha** captures: same word, written the way an English speaker hears it. *Bushia* is a hybrid of the two spellings. So *Busia* may be the most American way to say grandma in Polish, but its roots are very likely Polish.

### Babci, Baba and Gigi

**Babci** is another common family name in the US. In Polish it is not the basic form but a case form: genitive, dative or locative, as in *do babci* (to grandma's). A likely explanation is that children heard phrases like *idziemy do babci* ("we're going to grandma's") so often that the ending stuck. Some families also use **Babciu**, the vocative, as a name.

**Baba** is trickier. In several Slavic languages it is a normal word for grandma, and it is easy to see how it entered some immigrant families. In modern Polish, though, *baba* mostly means "woman" in a colloquial and often unflattering way, while its old "grandmother" meaning is marked as archaic. So calling your grandmother *Baba* in front of relatives in Poland may raise an eyebrow, and it is not the safe choice if you want a neutral word for grandma in Polish.

**Gigi** is not Polish at all. It is a general American grandma nickname, and if your Polish family uses it, it probably arrived from English.

### Dziadzia, Jaju and Other Spellings for Grandpa

Grandfathers get the same treatment. *Dziadzia* and *dziadziu* are real Polish affectionate forms, but their spelling is hard for English speakers, so families improvised. Harvard Magazine's profile of Jaju Pierogi, a company founded by two Polish-American sisters, explains that *Jaju* is the phonetic spelling of *dziadziu*, the name they used for their grandfather.

You will also see spellings like *Jaja* or *Dzia Dzia* on family cards. One friendly warning: in standard Polish, *jaja* means "eggs" (and is also slang for a joke), so if you write to relatives in Poland, *Dziadziu* will land more smoothly.

### Why the Family Word Differs From the Dictionary

Heritage words drift for simple reasons. Many families stopped speaking Polish at home after one or two generations, so a single word for grandma in Polish survived on its own, without the grammar around it. Children's pronunciations became permanent names. Spellings were invented by people who had never been taught Polish orthography. The result is a small, very personal vocabulary that is entirely authentic, just not standard.

If you are learning Polish now, you can keep both: call your own grandmother *Busia* forever, and use *babcia*, the standard grandma in Polish, when you talk with Poles about grandmothers in general.

## Babushka Is Russian, Not Polish

A common mix-up is to treat *babushka* as the word for grandma in Polish. It isn't. **Бабушка** (*babushka*) is the Russian word for grandmother. English borrowed it in the 1800s, and in American English it also came to mean a headscarf tied under the chin, and even a Russian nesting doll.

The confusion is understandable: *babushka*, *babusia* and *babcia* all grow from the same Slavic root, *baba*. But a Pole would never call their grandmother *babuszka*, and the headscarf in Polish is simply a *chustka*. If you want to sound Polish, leave *babushka* to Russian speakers: the real grandma in Polish is *babcia*.

## Dzień Babci and Dzień Dziadka: Grandparents' Days in Poland

Poland has two days set aside for grandparents, one right after the other:

- **Dzień Babci** (Grandma's Day) — **21 January**
- **Dzień Dziadka** (Grandpa's Day) — **22 January**

According to [Polish Wikipedia's article on Dzień Babci](https://pl.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dzie%C5%84_Babci), the idea first appeared in the weekly magazine *Kobieta i Życie* in 1964, after Mieczysława Ćwiklińska played a grandmother in the stage play *Drzewa umierają stojąc*. It was promoted by the newspaper *Express Poznański* in 1965, and in 1966 *Express Wieczorny* declared 21 January "grandma's day." Grandpa's Day came later and, interestingly for diaspora readers, Polish Wikipedia says it was probably brought over from America in the 1980s.

The customs are simple: visits, wishes, small gifts, flowers, performances by the grandchildren, and a **laurka**, the handmade card that Polish children make for parents and grandparents. If you have a grandmother in Poland, a call on 21 January will be noticed.

### Greetings for Dzień Babci and Dzień Dziadka

These phrases work in a card, a text or a phone call, and each one uses the right form of grandma in Polish or grandpa in Polish:

| Polish | English |
|---|---|
| *Wszystkiego najlepszego z okazji Dnia Babci!* | All the best on Grandma's Day! |
| *Wszystkiego najlepszego z okazji Dnia Dziadka!* | All the best on Grandpa's Day! |
| *Kochana Babciu, dużo zdrowia i uśmiechu!* | Dear Grandma, lots of health and smiles! |
| *Kochany Dziadku, dziękuję Ci za wszystko.* | Dear Grandpa, thank you for everything. |
| *Sto lat, Babciu!* | A hundred years, Grandma! (the classic Polish "many happy returns") |
| *Kocham Cię, Babciu.* | I love you, Grandma. |

Notice the vocative again: *Babciu*, *Dziadku*. And in letters, Poles traditionally capitalize *Ci* and *Cię* when addressing the reader directly, as a mark of respect and warmth.

## Scenario: Calling Babcia on Grandma's Day

You live in the US, your grandmother lives in Poland, and it is 21 January. Here is a simple, realistic call that puts several forms of grandma in Polish to work.

- **Ty (You):** *Cześć, babciu! Wszystkiego najlepszego z okazji Dnia Babci!* — Hi, Grandma! Happy Grandma's Day!
- **Babcia:** *Dziękuję, kochanie! Jak miło, że dzwonisz.* — Thank you, darling! How nice that you're calling.
- **Ty:** *Jak się czujesz?* — How are you feeling?
- **Babcia:** *Dobrze, dobrze. A kiedy przyjedziesz?* — Fine, fine. So when are you coming?
- **Ty:** *Latem przyjadę do ciebie z mamą.* — I'll come to see you in the summer with Mom.
- **Babcia:** *Cudownie! Upiekę szarlotkę.* — Wonderful! I'll bake an apple cake.
- **Ty:** *Buziaki dla dziadka! Jutro zadzwonię do niego.* — Kisses for Grandpa! I'll call him tomorrow.

Short, warm and grammatically correct. Notice *dla dziadka* and *do niego*: both prepositions take the genitive case.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the word for grandma in Polish?

The standard word for grandma in Polish is *babcia* (BAHP-chah). Affectionate versions include *babunia* and *babusia*. When you speak directly to her, use the vocative *babciu*.

### How do you pronounce grandma in Polish?

Say BAHP-chah. The *b* sounds almost like a *p* because it sits next to *c*, and *ci* is a soft "ch." The vocative *babciu*, used when you speak to her, sounds like BAHP-choo.

### Is there a different word for grandma in Polish on mom's side and dad's side?

No. Polish uses *babcia* for both grandmothers and *dziadek* for both grandfathers. To be specific, say *babcia ze strony mamy* (mom's side) or *babcia ze strony taty* (dad's side).

### How do you say grandpa in Polish?

Grandpa is *dziadek* (JAH-dek). Affectionate forms are *dziadzio* and *dziadziuś*, and when you call him you say *dziadku* or *dziadziu*. Grandparents together are *dziadkowie*.

### Is busia a real Polish word?

*Busia* is a real Polish-American family word, but it is not standard Polish and you won't find it in Polish dictionaries with that meaning. It most likely began as a child's shortening of *babusia*. In Poland, the usual word for grandma in Polish is *babcia*.

### Is babushka Polish?

No. *Babushka* is Russian for grandmother. The word for grandma in Polish is *babcia*, which comes from the same Slavic root but is a different word.

### Why do some people say "babci" instead of "babcia"?

*Babci* is a case form of *babcia*, used after words like *do* (to) and *u* (at). Phrases such as *do babci* ("to grandma's") are so common that some heritage families adopted *Babci* as their everyday name for grandma in Polish.

### When is Grandma's Day in Poland?

Dzień Babci falls on 21 January, and Dzień Dziadka (Grandpa's Day) on 22 January. Both are celebrated every year, especially by schoolchildren.

### What is the Polish word for great-grandma?

Great-grandma is *prababcia*, and great-grandpa is *pradziadek*. Add another *pra-* for each extra generation: *praprababcia* is a great-great-grandmother.

Whatever your family called her, Busia, Babci or plain *babcia*, the word for grandma in Polish carries the same mix of soup, stories and quiet authority. Learning the standard form and its endings doesn't replace the family word. It just lets you use both, and maybe surprise her with a perfectly formed *Kochana Babciu* on the next 21 January.
