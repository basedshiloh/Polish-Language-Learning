The textbook way to ask how are you in Polish is *Jak się masz?* (yahk sheh mahsh), but in everyday conversation you're just as likely to hear *Co słychać?* (tsoh SWY-hahch), *Co u ciebie?* or a quick *Jak leci?* Each one has a slightly different flavour, and the formal versions change shape entirely once *Pan* or *Pani* enters the sentence.

The bigger surprise for English speakers is the answer. In English, "How are you?" is often a greeting that expects "Fine, thanks." In Poland, the question tends to be taken more literally. Ask a Polish friend how they are and you may get a real update: the bad night's sleep, the annoying boss, the knee that's acting up again.

This guide covers all the common ways to ask how are you in Polish, with phonetic spellings, a full scale of answers from *świetnie* to *kiepsko*, the gendered forms that trip up learners, and how to return the question politely. You'll also find the cultural background to that honest answer, the mistakes that give learners away, and a short dialogue to model your own small talk on.

## Jak Się Masz: The Textbook How Are You in Polish

*Jak się masz?* is the how are you in Polish that every course teaches first, and it's correct, understood everywhere and perfectly friendly. Some Poles find it a touch bookish in casual conversation and reach for *co słychać* or *co u ciebie* instead, but you'll still hear it often, especially with children, within families and in warm, slightly old-fashioned speech.

### What jak się masz literally means

Word for word, it's "how do you have yourself?" *Jak* is "how," *masz* is "you have," and *się* is the reflexive "yourself." The verb *mieć się* (to be doing, literally "to have oneself") is a reflexive verb, and like many Polish reflexives it means something you couldn't guess from the parts. If *się* still puzzles you, our guide to [reflexive verbs in Polish](/blog/reflexive-verbs-in-polish) breaks down what it's doing in phrases like this one.

Because it's built on *mieć*, the question changes with the person you're asking:

| Polish | Sounds like | Asking about |
|---|---|---|
| Jak się masz? | yahk sheh mahsh | You (one friend) |
| Jak się macie? | yahk sheh MAH-cheh | You all (friends) |
| Jak się ma Kasia? | yahk sheh mah KAH-shah | Kasia (a third person) |
| Jak się mają dzieci? | yahk sheh MAH-yon JEH-chee | The children |
| Jak się Pan ma? | yahk sheh pahn mah | You (a man, formal) |
| Jak się Państwo mają? | yahk sheh PAHN-stfoh MAH-yon | You (a couple or group, formal) |

### Jak się masz pronunciation tips

The *się* is the sound learners most often get wrong. It's not "see" or "sigh" but **sheh**, with a soft "sh" and a nasal *ę* that, at the end of a word, usually relaxes to a plain "eh." Run the three words together as one unit, **yahk-sheh-MAHSH**, with the stress on *masz*.

## Co Słychać: The Everyday How Are You in Polish

*Co słychać?* (**tsoh SWY-hahch**) is probably the most natural way to ask how are you in Polish in daily life. It literally means "what's to be heard?" The verb *słychać* has a second meaning, which the [PWN dictionary entry for *słychać*](https://sjp.pwn.pl/sjp/s%C5%82ycha%C4%87;2521996.html) gives as something "known from hearsay," so the question is really "what's the news?"

That literal meaning shapes the answers. *Co słychać?* invites news rather than a report on your health: what's new at work, how the family is, what you did at the weekend. It works with friends, neighbours and colleagues, and you can make it formal by adding *u* + *Pana* or *Pani*.

| Polish | Sounds like | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Co słychać? | tsoh SWY-hahch | What's new? / How are things? |
| Co u ciebie słychać? | tsoh oo CHEH-byeh SWY-hahch | What's new with you? |
| Co słychać u Pana? | tsoh SWY-hahch oo PAH-nah | How are things with you? (to a man, formal) |
| Co słychać u Pani? | tsoh SWY-hahch oo PAH-nee | How are things with you? (to a woman, formal) |
| Co u was słychać? | tsoh oo vahs SWY-hahch | What's new with you all? |

Pronunciation note: *ł* is an English "w," and *ch* is the throaty "h" of Scottish "loch." Say **SWY-hahch**, never "sly-chatch."

## Co U Ciebie, Jak Leci and Co Tam: Casual How Are You in Polish

Between friends, the short and slangy versions of how are you in Polish dominate. They're relaxed, quick, and often come straight after a *cześć* or *siema*.

**Co u ciebie?** (**tsoh oo CHEH-byeh**) means "what's with you?" The little word *u* means "at" or "with," like French *chez*, and *ciebie* is the form of *ty* (you) that follows it. To a group, it becomes *Co u was?*

**Jak leci?** (**yahk LEH-chee**) literally means "how's it flying?" and works like "how's it going?" It's cheerful and casual. The classic answer, *jakoś leci*, means "it's flying somehow," or "getting by."

**Co tam?** (**tsoh tahm**) and **Jak tam?** (**yahk tahm**) are the Polish "what's up?" They're very common in texts, phone calls and quick hallway chats. You'll often hear them stacked: *Siema, co tam?*

**Co nowego?** (**tsoh noh-VEH-goh**) means "what's new?" and is useful when you haven't seen someone for a while.

**Jak życie?** (**yahk ZHY-cheh**, "how's life?") is casual and a little jokey, a nice change if you've asked *co słychać* three times this week.

## Formal Ways to Ask How Are You in Polish

With your boss, an older neighbour, a teacher, your partner's parents or a client, you need the formal how are you in Polish. Polish doesn't have a formal "you" pronoun as such; instead you talk to the person as *Pan* (sir) or *Pani* (madam), and the verb switches to the third person.

So *Jak się masz?* (how are you, informal) becomes *Jak się Pan ma?* to a man and *Jak się Pani ma?* to a woman. In the same way, *Co u ciebie?* becomes *Co u Pana?* or *Co u Pani?* For a couple or a mixed group, use *Państwo*: *Jak się Państwo mają?*

A slightly more elegant, old-fashioned option is *Jak się Pan miewa?* or *Jak się Pani miewa?* (**MYEH-vah**), from *miewać się*, "to be keeping." You'll hear it from older speakers and in polite letters.

Is there a special way to ask how are you in Polish to a girl? Not in the question itself: *Jak się masz?* and *Co u ciebie?* are the same for everyone. The difference is formal versus informal. A girl or young woman you know gets *Co u ciebie?*; an older woman or a stranger gets *Co u Pani słychać?* The gender difference shows up in the answers, as you'll see below.

Whichever formal how are you in Polish you choose, keep the *Pan* or *Pani* all the way through the conversation. The grammar reference on common questions and answers drills exactly this pattern, including how the case of the answer follows from the question word.

## How to Answer How Are You in Polish: From Świetnie to Kiepsko

Here's a realistic scale of answers, from great to rough. These are adverbs and set phrases, so they don't change with your gender.

|---|---|---|
| Świetnie! | SHVYET-nyeh | Great! |
| Super! | SOO-per | Super! |
| Bardzo dobrze | BAR-dzoh DOB-zheh | Very well |
| Dobrze, dziękuję | DOB-zheh jen-KOO-yeh | Fine, thank you |
| W porządku | f poh-ZHOND-koo | Okay / all right |
| Wszystko gra | FSHIST-koh grah | All good (literally "everything plays") |
| Nie narzekam | nyeh nah-ZHEH-kahm | Can't complain |
| Może być | MOH-zheh bych | It'll do / okay-ish |

| Bywało lepiej | by-VAH-woh LEH-pyey | I've been better |

In practice, the middle of this table gets the most use. *Dobrze*, *w porządku*, *nie narzekam* and *jakoś leci* are the everyday answers. *Świetnie* and *super* are genuine, but said by reflex every time, they can sound a bit like an advert.

For *co słychać*, which asks for news rather than wellbeing, you can also answer with *Nic nowego* (nothing new), *Wszystko po staremu* (everything's the same as ever) or *Dużo się dzieje* (a lot is going on), then actually share something.

Some of the most natural answers to how are you in Polish use adjectives, and Polish adjectives agree with the speaker's gender. A man and a woman say the same idea with different endings.

The same agreement applies when you ask: *Jesteś zmęczony?* to a man, *Jesteś zmęczona?* to a woman (are you tired?).

## The Honest Answer: Why Poles Don't Just Say "Fine"

Many foreigners in Poland notice the same thing: ask how are you in Polish and you may get a real answer, complaints included. The British writer Jamie Stokes described this in [an essay on Polish complaining for the news portal WP](https://wiadomosci.wp.pl/the-art-of-polish-complaining-6036301044085889a), recalling a friend who answered his greeting with a five-minute account of his aching feet and the state of the world. In his telling, complaining wasn't rude at all; it invited people to share their own grievances.

Polish psychologists have discussed the same habit. Social psychologist Bogdan Wojciszke argued that Poles have built a "culture of complaining" (*kultura narzekania*), in which it's normal, even expected, to speak about the world in negative terms, and he contrasted it with the American "keep smiling" norm. His thesis has been debated, and many younger, urban Poles answer far more breezily, but the pattern is real enough that learners should be ready for it.

![Two older men in jackets sitting side by side on a park bench in the sun, deep in conversation, the kind of unhurried chat where how are you in Polish gets a real answer — Photo by Ayşegül Aytören on Pexels](/blog/how-are-you-in-polish-park-bench.webp)

What does that mean when you ask how are you in Polish yourself?

- **Ask only if you have a moment.** A "how are you?" tossed out while walking past someone tends to be rarer in Poland than in English-speaking countries. A simple *dzień dobry* or *cześć* is fine for passing hellos.
- **Listen and react.** If someone answers *kiepsko*, a quick *Oj, co się stało?* (Oh, what happened?) or *Współczuję* (**fspoow-CHOO-yeh**, I'm sorry to hear that) is kind and natural.
- **Don't oversell your own day.** A modest *dobrze* or *nie narzekam* usually sits more comfortably than an enthusiastic "Amazing!" every single time.
- **Notice the irony.** *Nie narzekam* literally means "I'm not complaining," and it's often said with a little smile for exactly that reason.

## Returning the Question: A Ty?

Answering how are you in Polish usually ends by bouncing the question back, just as English speakers say "Good, you?" Polish does it with a tiny *a*, meaning "and."

| Polish | Sounds like | When |
|---|---|---|
| A ty? | ah ty | And you? (informal) |
| A u ciebie? | ah oo CHEH-byeh | And with you? (after *co u ciebie?*) |
| A co u ciebie? | ah tsoh oo CHEH-byeh | And what's new with you? |
| A Pan? / A Pani? | ah pahn / ah PAH-nee | And you? (formal) |
| A u Pana? / A u Pani? | ah oo PAH-nah / ah oo PAH-nee | And with you? (formal) |
| A Państwo? | ah PAHN-stfoh | And you? (formal, group) |

The full pattern is short and very common: *Dobrze, dziękuję. A ty?* (Fine, thanks. And you?). The usual reply is *Też dobrze* (also good) or *U mnie też w porządku* (all right with me too). Match the form of the question: if someone asked you *Jak się Pani ma?*, answer and return it with *A Pan?* or *A Pani?*, not *A ty?*

## Common Mistakes When Asking How Are You in Polish

1. **Saying "Jestem dobrze."** It's a word-for-word copy of "I'm good," and it doesn't work in Polish. Say just *Dobrze* or *Mam się dobrze*. *Jestem dobry* means "I'm a good person" or "I'm good at something."

2. **Answering with *dobry* instead of *dobrze*.** *Dobry* is the adjective ("good"); *dobrze* is the adverb ("well"). The answer is always *dobrze*.

3. **Using *Jak się masz?* with your professor or boss.** It's informal. Use *Jak się Pan ma?* or *Co u Pani słychać?*

4. **Mixing forms.** *Jak się Pani masz?* combines formal *Pani* with the informal verb. With *Pan* or *Pani*, the verb is *ma*.

5. **Forgetting gender agreement.** A woman says *Jestem zmęczona*, not *zmęczony*.

6. **Expecting a one-word answer.** Leave room for a real reply, and be ready to say *Współczuję* or *To super!* (That's great!).

## Scenario: Bumping Into a Friend After the Holidays

It's early January. You run into your friend Ola outside a café in Wrocław. She asks how are you in Polish, you answer, and the conversation turns honest, as it often does.

> **Ola:** *Cześć! Kopę lat! Co u ciebie?*
> (Hi! Long time no see! How are you?)
>
> **You:** *Cześć! Dobrze, nie narzekam. Trochę zmęczony po świętach. A ty?*
> (Hi! Good, can't complain. A bit tired after the holidays. And you?)
>
> **Ola:** *Szczerze? Kiepsko. Mam grypę od tygodnia, a w pracy dramat.*
> (Honestly? Pretty rough. I've had flu for a week, and work is a drama.)
>
> **You:** *Oj, współczuję. Jak się teraz czujesz?*
> (Oh, I'm sorry to hear that. How are you feeling now?)
>
> **Ola:** *Już lepiej, dzięki. A co słychać u Tomka?*
> (Better now, thanks. And what's new with Tomek?)
>
> **You:** *Po staremu. Pracuje i gra w piłkę.*
> (Same as ever. Working and playing football.)

Notice how Ola gives a real answer instead of "fine," and how *współczuję* followed by a follow-up question keeps the conversation warm. If you were a woman, you'd say *Trochę zmęczona po świętach* instead. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common way to say how are you in Polish?

In everyday life, *Co słychać?* and *Co u ciebie?* are probably heard most among friends, while *Jak się masz?* is the classic textbook form that everyone understands. Formally, the most common way to say how are you in Polish is *Jak się Pan ma?* or *Jak się Pani ma?*

### How do you say how are you in Polish informally?

Use *Jak się masz?* (yahk sheh mahsh), *Co słychać?* (tsoh SWY-hahch) or *Co u ciebie?* (tsoh oo CHEH-byeh). Among close friends, *Jak leci?* and *Co tam?* are even more casual.

### How do you say how are you in Polish formally?

To a man, say *Jak się Pan ma?* or *Co słychać u Pana?* To a woman, *Jak się Pani ma?* or *Co słychać u Pani?* For a couple or group, *Jak się Państwo mają?*

### How do you respond to "jak się masz"?

The everyday answers are *Dobrze, dziękuję* (fine, thanks), *W porządku* (okay), *Nie narzekam* (can't complain) or *Jakoś leci* (getting by). Then return the question with *A ty?* (and you?).

### What does "co słychać" mean?

Literally "what's to be heard?" In practice it means "what's new?" or "how are things?" It invites news, so answers like *Nic nowego* (nothing new) or *Po staremu* (same as ever) fit well.

### How do you say how are you in Polish to a girl?

The question is the same for everyone: *Co u ciebie?* or *Jak się masz?* if you're on first-name terms, *Jak się Pani ma?* if not. Gender shows up in adjective answers, so she would say *Jestem zmęczona* (I'm tired), while a man says *zmęczony*.

### Is it rude to answer "kiepsko" when asked how are you in Polish?

Not at all. Honest answers are normal in Poland, and saying *kiepsko* (pretty bad) or *bywało lepiej* (I've been better) to a friend is perfectly acceptable. With a client or a stranger, a neutral *dobrze, dziękuję* is still the safer choice.

### What does "jak leci" mean?

Literally "how is it flying?" It's a casual "how's it going?" used between friends. The classic reply is *jakoś leci*, "getting by."

Knowing how to ask how are you in Polish is really about two things: matching the question to the relationship, and being ready to hear a genuine answer. Start with *co słychać* for friends and *jak się Pan ma* for everyone else, keep *a ty?* on the tip of your tongue, and don't be surprised when someone actually tells you about their week.
